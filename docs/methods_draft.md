# Materials and Methods (draft, 2026-09-15)

Sections 1–6 are the current docx text, lightly edited where numbers changed. Sections 7–12 are new and written from the committed scripts under `final_analyses/`; each ends with the script that produces the numbers.

## 1. Dataset identification and input preparation

HAMLET processes proteomics datasets identified by their ProteomeXchange accession (PXD). The production corpus comprised 1,737 human PRIDE datasets. Input text was assembled from the abstract, the materials-and-methods section where an open-access full text could be retrieved, the PRIDE project properties, and the PRIDE sample- and data-processing protocols. A PubMed abstract was preferred when available (296 datasets), otherwise the abstract supplied through PRIDE (1,441). Extracted methods were available for 601 datasets (open-access condition); the remaining 1,136 had abstract text only (closed-access condition). Sample- and data-processing protocols were available for 1,727 datasets. Text was parsed into separately labelled blocks; introduction, results, supplementary text and pre-existing SDRF summaries were excluded. A preparation manifest recorded accession, input provenance, section availability, section lengths and input hashes.

## 2. Agent-based extraction

Extraction was implemented in DocETL with three specialised agents for biological characteristics, mass-spectrometry procedure and experimental design, covering 38 fields. Each agent used a fixed prompt with field definitions, extraction rules, worked examples, evidence requirements and the output schema. Values were restricted to the deposited experiment; inference was allowed only where a field's instructions permitted it. Every field was a list of {value, evidence} objects so that heterogeneous datasets could carry several organisms, tissues, instruments or conditions; missing information was represented explicitly as unknown. PRIDE properties were treated as dataset-level descriptors and had to be cited by their complete property line.

## 3. Model inference

Production runs used Qwen3.8-27B served through vLLM 0.27.1 (BF16, one NVIDIA B300 per server, tensor-parallel size 1, 65,536-token context, GPU-memory utilisation 0.70), with the corpus processed in eight shards. The cumulative ablation additionally used Gemma 4 31B (`google/gemma-4-31B-it`, BF16, 32,768-token context, TRITON attention backend) and GLM-4.7, all at temperature 0 with thinking disabled. DocETL requests timed out after 600 s without automatic retry; targeted repair runs used 1,800 s. Timeouts governed execution only, never the acceptance of a value.

## 4. Ontology normalisation

Extracted values were normalised against 19 ontologies and controlled vocabularies (CL, CLO, UBERON, a curated NCBITaxon subset, DOID, Mondo, PSI-MS, PSI-MOD, UNIMOD, BTO, PRIDE-CV, EFO, ChEBI, PATO, HANCESTRO and organism-specific anatomy resources). Deterministic lexical matches on primary labels and exact synonyms took precedence. Remaining values were embedded with SapBERT (`cambridgeltl/SapBERT-from-PubMedBERT-fulltext`, final-layer [CLS] token, L2-normalised, 128-token maximum) and matched by FAISS nearest-neighbour search over ontology labels and synonyms (five candidates, acceptance at cosine ≥ 0.70). Raw and normalised values were stored side by side.

## 5. Evidence grounding and quality control

Evidence quotations were located in the input text, with minor typographical differences reconciled; ungrounded evidence and values with missing evidence were flagged. Three checks produced diagnostic flags without deleting values: ontology-supported cross-field consistency (cell line–species, cell–disease–anatomy), NegEx negation detection through negspacy, and numeric agreement between an extracted quantity and its evidence.

## 6. Output auditing and recovery

Production outputs were audited for dataset coverage, schema validity, evidence grounding and preservation of multi-value entries. Timed-out requests were recovered from the DocETL cache by exact request key and passed through the same evidence, normalisation and QC steps; only missing output files were added.

## 7. Expert annotation study (Figure 1)

Twenty-seven open-access manuscripts were annotated in brat by nine EuBIC-MS annotators: one expert covering all 27 papers and eight annotators covering overlapping batches, plus a curated consensus per paper. The three models annotated the same abstract-plus-methods text in the S0 single-prompt configuration (Section 9). Of 68 raw entity labels, 42 were retained: tagged by the consensus in at least three papers and by at least two distinct annotators. Presence agreement was Cohen's kappa on binary per-paper, per-label indicators over the papers a pair shared. Because most slots are empty, kappa was decomposed into raw agreement, chance agreement, the both-absent share and positive specific agreement (2·n11/(2·n11+n10+n01)); model–model against human–human differences were tested by an exact permutation over which raters are models (C(12,3) = 220 assignments). Value-level agreement used two schemes: exact string identity, and clusters of values within SapBERT cosine 0.70. The threshold was taken from the normaliser rather than tuned; on a negative control of 88,226 value pairs from unrelated fields SapBERT never exceeded 0.49, whereas mean-pooled SciBERT reached 0.70 on 18.2% of them, which is why SciBERT was replaced throughout. Rarity was the share of papers in which the consensus stated the field; its relation to difficulty was tested by Spearman correlation over human pairs. (`final_analyses/HumanAnnotation/scripts/build_tables.py`, `build_figure1.py`; full derivations in `METHODS.md`.)

## 8. Benchmark ground truth and matching

Prompts were developed on 107 datasets with community SDRF-Proteomics files and evaluated on 30 held-out datasets. Golden values were derived per agent and field from the SDRF (12 datasets) or from curated annotation files in the same schema (18 datasets); taxon-identifier prefixes in organism values were stripped during conversion. Six fields not reported in the manuscript (material type, PTM, technology type, developmental stage, ethnicity, mass analyser) were excluded, leaving 16 fields and 179 golden values per run.

Each extracted value was compared with its golden value by a five-level matcher: exact string; normalised string (case and trademark symbols); identical ontology term; ancestor–descendant relation within four is_a edges in CL or UBERON; SapBERT cosine ≥ 0.70. Numeric fields (replicate, sample and fraction counts, collision energy) were compared as numbers and accepted only when equal, because SapBERT places unrelated small integers above the semantic threshold. Multi-valued fields were scored per golden value against the best extracted value, averaged. A golden value counted as a true positive when an extracted value was present and its match score reached 0.50, as a false negative when no value was extracted, and as both a false positive and a false negative when a value was present but did not match. Precision, recall and F1 were computed per agent and the overall F1 is the mean over the three agents. (`framework/benchmark/semantic_matcher.py`, `agentic-benchmark/benchmark_data/run_sdrf_benchmark.py`.)

For the ontology-backed fields (species, tissue, cell type, cell line, disease, instrument, cleavage agent, label) agreement was additionally graded by Lin similarity, 2·IC(MICA)/(IC(a)+IC(b)) with IC(c) = −log2((|descendants(c)|+1)/(N+1)), on the is_a (plus part_of for anatomy) graph of the relevant ontology, after resolving both values with the normaliser; cell types and cell lines were resolved against both CL and CLO. A missing or unresolvable extracted value scored 0; golden values that did not resolve were left out of the denominator. (`final_analyses/icgraph.py`, `SapBERTStaircase/scripts/build_figure2.py`.)

## 9. Cumulative ablation (Figure 2)

Six arms were run on the 30 test datasets with frozen prompts: S0, one master prompt returning bare extractions; S1, three specialised agents; S2, S1 plus field definitions, rules, examples and explicit unknown handling; S3, S2 plus structural validation with retries; S4, S3 plus gleaning; S5, S4 plus ontology normalisation. Inputs were restricted to title, abstract and methods. Each arm was run three times per model at temperature 0; figures report the mean and standard deviation over runs. Output volume is the number of non-unknown values per dataset; unanimity is the share of (dataset, field) slots whose normalised value was identical in all three runs. The composition of accepted values at S5 records which matcher level accepted each golden value. (`SapBERTStaircase/scripts/rescore_replicates.py`, `build_figure2.py`, `build_error_analysis.py`, `draw_figure2.py`.)

## 10. Comparison of input sources (Figure 4)

Three extractions of the 1,737 datasets were made with the frozen production pipeline: manuscript text only (abstract, plus methods where available), PRIDE project properties and protocols only, and both combined. Coverage was the number of populated fields and of distinct values per dataset, excluding unknowns, reported separately for the closed-access (1,135; PXD001017 excluded because its PRIDE-only extraction did not complete) and open-access (601) datasets. For the manuscript versus combined comparison, fields populated in both runs were compared value set against value set: lexical, numeric or unambiguous ontology equivalence; broader–narrower relation within four is_a edges; SapBERT candidate at cosine ≥ 0.70; partial overlap; no accepted overlap. The same pairs were graded with Lin similarity on the ontology-backed fields (Section 8), and the experimental-design fields with a field-appropriate rule: Lin on EFO for technology type and on EFO, Mondo, UBERON, CL and ChEBI for factor values; identity for the nine-value experimental-design vocabulary; and, for counts, equal (agree), within one (partial) or otherwise (disagree). Neither run was treated as ground truth and the two conditions are independent stochastic generations, so a dataset can lose fields when PRIDE text is added. Field counts include schema aliases. (`PRIDEContextGain1737/compare_three_levels.py`, `plot_three_sources_excluding_pxd001017.py`, `lin_agreement.py`, `expdesign_agreement.py`, `draw_figure4.py`.)

## 11. Expression-based tissue prediction and file-level resolution (Figure 5)

The 62,329 fraction-collapsed human samples of the reprocessing database (intensity-based NSAF) were scored with MLMarker (34 tissue classes, model TP_full_quant_update_20250113). Coverage is the share of MLMarker's 5,979 feature proteins quantified in a sample. Samples with coverage ≥ 0.10 were scored by the Random Forest's class probabilities; samples below it by the package's SHAP path with penalty factor 1, which removes the bone-marrow default that near-empty profiles otherwise receive. Only samples with coverage ≥ 0.10 were used for conclusions; the 183 MLMarker training projects were excluded from every comparison unless stated. Projects whose HAMLET tissue is only a biofluid were not assessable and were excluded from all denominators.

Concordance between HAMLET's tissue and MLMarker's top prediction was tiered by Lin similarity and UBERON graph distance: exact match; near agreement (Lin ≥ 0.70 or ≤ 2 edges); distant concordance (Lin ≥ 0.35 or ≤ 4 edges); conflict; and, separately, HAMLET tissues outside MLMarker's 34 classes. Calibration was assessed by binning the top-class probability. Healthy native and cancer/diseased cohorts were defined from HAMLET's disease state, species and material fields.

Where HAMLET listed several tissues for a project, each file was assigned the listed tissue ranking highest in MLMarker's top five; a file whose own top prediction was one of the listed tissues counted as top-1 agreement, one rescued from rank 2–5 as top-5 agreement. Assignments were validated against file-level labels that never derive from HAMLET: community SDRFs (PXD000561, PXD020192, PXD010154, PXD006401 human files) and a sample-to-tumour-type map (PXD048734); PXD010296 was dropped because its files are rat. Projects inside MLMarker's training set are shown but marked. (`MLMarkerPenalty/scripts/predict_mlmarker_coverage.py`, `MLMarker_v_HAMLET_penalty.ipynb`, `build_run_labels.py`, `granularity_and_disease.py`, `propose_choose_atlas.py`, `validate_disambiguation.py`, `draw_concordance_figure.py`, `draw_disambiguation_figure.py`.)

## 12. Software and figures

Python 3.12 with pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, PyTorch 2.10 (CPU) and Transformers 5.15 for SapBERT, FAISS for retrieval, Matplotlib for figures. All figures share one palette and style (`final_analyses/figure_style.py`): biological, technical and experimental design in blue, green and orange; models in purple, teal and brown; input sources in blue, orange and green.
