ABSTRACT:
The identification of reliable biomarkers is imperative for enhancing early detection, prognosis, and treatment of breast cancer. This study investigates a human telomeric dimeric G-quadruplex (G4) model, Tel46, which has been functionalized on Controlled Pore Glass (CPG) support, as a novel tool for biomarker discovery. Tel46 closely mimics multimeric G4 structures found in telomeric overhangs. Utilizing the affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS) approach, we identified 93 nuclear proteins that interacted with Tel46 in MCF7 breast cancer cells. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that these proteins were linked to DNA replication, repair, and genome stability pathways, which are frequently altered in cancer. To further refine these findings, we integrated AP-MS data with quantitative proteomics, comparing MCF7 and non-tumorigenic MCF10a cells. This approach led to the identification of 27 Tel46 interactors that were overexpressed. The subsequent functional analyses of these proteins revealed their enrichment in DNA replication, damage repair, and genome maintenance pathways, emphasising their potential as cancer biomarkers. Conversely, the downregulated proteins were associated with basic cellular functions such as protein synthesis and cytoskeletal organisation. Subsequent bioinformatics analyses incorporating public cancer genomics and transcriptomics data identified 19 potential biomarkers, including MSH6, MSH2, ESRP1, and WDHD1, which were further linked to poor clinical outcomes in breast cancer subtypes. This study validates the use of Tel46-functionalized CPG as a novel tool for isolating cancer-related proteins and highlights the potential of telomeric G4-interacting proteins as biomarkers for breast cancer diagnosis and therapy. These findings provide a foundation for future research into G4-mediated molecular mechanisms in cancer.

PROJECT DESCRIPTION:
Integrating Proteomics and Bioinformatics Methods for the Identification of Breast Cancer Biomarkers Interacting with Telomeric G-Quadruplex

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
Three independent pellets from MCF7 and MCF10a, respectively, were lysed by adding a 5% SDS, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution according to Palinski et al (72). Total proteins were digested with trypsin by using S-Trap cartridges (Protifi, Huntington, NY). The LC-MS/MS analysis of the peptide mixtures were carried out with an Orbitrap Exploris 240 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) mass spectrometer coupled with a VanquishNeo (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) chromatographer. The LC gradient was set by mixing solvent A (2% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water LC-MS grade) and B (2% water LC-MS grade, 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile) starting from 2% to 30% of B in 60 min and then from 30% to 50% of B in 5 min at 250µl/min flow rate. The mass spectrometry analysis was carried out in DDA mode by fragmenting the top 20 intense ions (73). The acquisition range spanned from 300 to 1800 m/z units, and the Orbitrap resolution was set to 120,000. The LC-MS/MS data were run in triplicates.

DATA PROCESSING:
Protein identification and quantification based on LC-MS/MS data were performed using MaxQuant (v 2.4.10), setting the search parameters as reported above. Statistical analyses were carried out by using Perseus software (v 1.6.15.0), to filter out the protein contaminants (i.e., reverse and potential contaminants) and those entries with more than 70% of not valid values in at least one group in the LFQ intensity column. The remaining zero values were imputed according to the Gaussian method (74). A Student’s t-test was performed with 1% Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p-value cutoff. The fold changes (FC) were calculated as the mean difference of the protein log2 LFQ intensities in MCF7 versus MCF10a. The differentially expressed proteins were considered those with log2FC<-0.5 and log2FC>0.5 as down- and up-regulated, respectively.

METHODS:
Methods
Preparation of oligonucleotide-functionalized CPG supports
The oligonucleotide-functionalized Controlled Pore Glass (CPG) supports were synthesized as previously described. Particularly, using standard phosphoramidite chemistry on an automated DNA synthesizer, the oligonucleotides of sequences d[(AGGG(TTAGGG)7] (tel46) and d(CGCGAATTCGCGTTTCGCGAATTCGCG) (ds27) were assembled on an ad hoc synthesized CPG support. The final oligonucleotide functionalization on the CPG supports was 2 and 4 nmol/mg for tel46-CPG and ds27-CPG, respectively, corresponding to 29 and 33 µg/mg of each oligonucleotide. Both CPG supports were subjected to an annealing procedure (75 °C for 5 min and slow cooling down to room temperature in 3 h, followed by storage at 4 °C until usage) in a buffer mimicking the intracellular conditions (10 mM potassium phosphate, 100 mM KCl, pH 7.4) favouring the thermodynamically preferred conformations of the oligonucleotides covalently attached to the solid support. Fishing of the oligonucleotide protein partners was then performed from the proper nuclear cellular lysate. ds27 (double stranded DNA) was employed during the pre-cleaning step to reduce the protein background, while tel46 was employed as bait for interactome purification.
Cell culture
Human breast adenocarcinoma cells MCF7 were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2. Human non-tumorigenic epithelial cells from mammary gland ATCC-CRL-10,317 MCF10A were cultured in complete Mammary Epithelial Growth Medium (MEGM) containing 100 ng/mL of cholera toxin at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2. To obtain a suitable amount of proteins for subsequent analyses, both cell lines were seeded into 100 mm petri plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/plate. At the end of cell amplification, ~ 40 × 106 of MCF7 and ~ 60 × 106 of MCF10A cells were obtained; they were centrifuged and then washed two times in PBS.
Cell Lysis
MCF7 cells were lysed according to a fractionated lysis protocol to separately obtain cytosolic and nuclear extracts. Briefly, buffer A (10 mM HEPES pH = 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM PMSF, 0.5% Nonidet P40, and protease inhibitor tablet) was added to the cell pellet in a v/v ratio 1:40 (pellet: buffer) and incubated for 15 min on ice. The solubilized pellet was gently shaken at 4 °C for 5 min. Following centrifugation (2,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min), the supernatant, consisting of cytosolic protein extract, was discarded, while the nuclear pellet was washed twice in buffer A without Nonidet P40, and then lysed in buffer B (20 mM HEPES pH = 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM PMSF, 0.05% Tween-20), v/v ratio 1:4 (pellet: buffer), left on ice for 15 min and then vortexed for 15 min. The nuclear extract was recovered after 30 min centrifugation at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C. The proteins were quantified by Bradford assay.
Affinity purification – mass spectrometry (AP-MS) analysis of CPG-tel46 interactome
CPG-ds27 support (42 µg of oligonucleotide) was suspended in MCF7 and MCF10A nuclear extracts (840 µg, each) in buffer B for 2 h at 4 °C, and the collected supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the CPG-tel46 support (36 µg of oligonucleotide) according to the AP-MS protocol. Following the overnight incubation under gentle agitation on a stirring wheel, the CPG-tel46 flowthrough was discarded and both CPG-ds27 and CPG-tel46 were washed three times with buffer B supplemented with 100 mM KCl to remove potential contaminants. The specific oligonucleotides interactors were eluted with 5% SDS in ammonium bicarbonate 50 mM, pH 8, by 10 min incubation at 99 °C. CPG-ds27 retained proteins were stored and used as control. The pulldown eluates were subjected to a proteomics shotgun procedure, consisting in a direct trypsinization of eluted protein samples on S-Trap cartridges (Protifi, Huntington, NY) according to the vendor protocol. The peptide mixtures were resuspended in 0.2% formic acid and analyzed by nanoLC-MS/MS using the Easy-nLC II chromatographic system coupled with a LTQ Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Chromatographic fractionations were carried out onto a C18 capillary column (5 μm biosphere, 75 μm internal diameter, 200 mm length) using a non-linear gradient, from 5 to 50% of eluent B (95% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid) in A (5% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water), over 260 min. Mass analyses were performed in Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA) mode by fragmenting the 10 most intense ions in CID modality. The acquisition range spanned from 300 to 1800 m/z units, and the Orbitrap resolution was set to 60,000.
Protein identification was carried out by MaxQuant software (v.1.5.2.8), using UniProt Homo Sapiens as database for Andromeda search. Parameters used for protein identification were the following: (i) a minimum of 3 peptides, including at least 1 unique; (ii) variable allowed modifications were methionine oxidation and pyroglutamate formation on N-terminal glutamine; (iii) accuracy for the first search was set to 10 ppm, then lowered to 5 ppm in the main search; (iv) 0.01 FDR was used with a reverse database for decoy. The CPG-tel46 -binding identified proteins here identified, depleted from those shared with the CPG-ds27 dataset, represented the specific interactors of tel46. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD059593. To get rid of possible contaminants, the list was subjected to analysis with Crapome database by using the available option “Workflow 1” and selecting “H. Sapiens – All” as database. The interactors identified in both cell lines were filtered to remove those present in more than 40% of the experiments already reported with the selected database. The functional enrichment analyses were performed with Panther database (version 18.0 Released 2023-08-01) platform consulting cellular component, molecular functions, and biological processes gene ontology databases (Released 26-02-2024). The analyses were performed by applying a Fisher exact test and using 5% FDR as cutoff (Benjamini-Hochberg corrected). The output terms were filtered by fold enrichment ≥ 5. The plot was obtained by SRPlot tool.
Label free differential proteomics experiments
Three independent pellets from MCF7 and MCF10A, respectively, were lysed by adding a 5% SDS, 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution according to Cozzolino et al.. Total proteins were digested with trypsin by using S-Trap cartridges (Protifi, Huntington, NY). The LC-MS/MS analysis of the peptide mixtures were carried out with an Orbitrap Exploris 240 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) mass spectrometer coupled with a VanquishNeo (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) UHPLC system. The LC gradient was set by mixing solvent A (2% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water LC-MS grade) and B (2% water LC-MS grade, 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile) starting from 2 to 30% of B in 60 min and then from 30 to 50% of B in 5 min at 250 µl/min flow rate. The mass spectrometry analysis was carried out in DDA mode by fragmenting the top 20 intense ions. The acquisition range spanned from 300 to 1800 m/z units, and the Orbitrap resolution was set to 120,000. The LC-MS/MS data were run in triplicates. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD059597.
Protein identification and quantification based on LC-MS/MS data were performed using MaxQuant (v 2.4.10), setting the search parameters as reported above. Statistical analyses were carried out by using Perseus software (v 1.6.15.0), to filter out the protein contaminants (i.e., reverse and potential contaminants) and those entries with more than 70% of not valid values in at least one group in the LFQ intensity column. The remaining zero values were imputed according to the Gaussian method. A Student’s t-test was performed with 1% Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p-value cutoff. The fold changes (FC) were calculated as the mean difference of the protein log2 LFQ intensities in MCF7 versus MCF10A. The differentially expressed proteins were considered those with log2FC<−0.5 and log2FC > 0.5 as down- and up-regulated, respectively. The functional enrichment was performed for the up- and down- regulated proteins separately according to Panther database, as mentioned in paragraph “Affinity purification – mass spectrometry (AP-MS) analysis of CPG-tel46 interactome”. The ontologies with fold enrichment > 5, FDR < 0.01 and protein counts > 3 were reported in the analysis. The plots were obtained by SRPlot tool.
UALCAN
UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) is based on publicly available cancer OMICS datasets. We interrogated the UALCAN database to find the protein expression levels of interactomics-derived proteins according to Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) dataset. The proteins were considered differentially expressed between normal and BC condition when p-value was less than 1E-04.
Gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA) dataset
The GEPIA interactive web server (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/detail.php) was used to evaluate the transcriptional levels of the 19 proteomics-derived candidates comparing normal and breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) tissues according to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype Tissue Expression (GTEx) datasets. The analysis was performed by using a LIMMA test, selecting a q-value cutoff 0.01 and |Log2FC|>0.5.
Breast cancer gene-expression miner v5.0 (bc-GenExMiner v5.0) online tool
Bc-GenExMiner (http://bcgenex.ico.unicancer.fr/BC-GEM/GEM-Accueil.php?js=1) online tool hosts curated breast cancer transcriptomic data from published sources, including DNA microarrays (n = 11,552) and RNA-seq (n = 5,023). Users can conduct various analyses, named as prognostic, correlation, and expression studies. In our investigation, we used the expression correlation module within “targeted” analysis to assess the linear expression correlation among the 19 candidate genes. Specifically, we focused on the DNA microarray dataset and evaluated combinations of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status. Additionally, we utilized the “expression” module to examine the expression levels of the 19 candidates relative to clinicopathological and molecular characteristics of the population documented in the database.
Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis
Kaplan-Meier plotter (KM plotter, https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=background) correlates the expression of a gene and the patients survival in more than 350,000 samples from 21 tumour types. It applies statistical tools including Cox proportional hazards regression and the computation of the False Discovery Rate. The analysis was carried out by using the mRNA expression data in BC. In particular, the correlation between the target gene mRNA expression levels and relapse-free survival (RFS), the overall survival (OS) rate, distance metastasis free survival (DMFS) in BC groups were calculated by the Kaplan–Meier curve and log-rank test. The software calculated the hazard ratio (HR) value and the 95% confidence. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant according to the Log-rank test.
Recombinant proteins pulldowns using CPG-tel46 resin
To test the interaction between G4-tel46 and the proteins candidate as potential biomarkers, a pulldown assay was set up. For pre-cleaning, naked CPG support (1 mg) suspended in 100 µL of annealing buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 150 mM NaCl, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.05% Tween-20) was used. The suspended support (20 µL) was respectively incubated with 4 µg of each recombinant proteins, and kept 1 h at 4 °C on a stirring wheel. Subsequently, the unbound fraction was incubated with the same amount of CPG-tel46 suspension agitated at 4 °C overnight. The proteins were then eluted from the supports (CPG and CPG-tel46) with 5%SDS, 50 mM AMBIC at 99 °C under stirring for 10 min. The elution of naked CPG (pre-cleaning) and CPG-G4 as well as 2 µg of protein loading control were fractionated by 8% SDS-PAGE. The gel was stained with GelCode™ Blue Safe Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and destained with Milli-Q water.
Multiple reaction monitoring
Triplicates of peptide mixtures from MCF7 and MCF10A cells were obtained as already reported in “Label free differential proteomics experiments” paragraph. For each protein, transitions were predicted by using Skyline v 25.1.0.142 (MacCoss Lab Software, Dept of Genome Sciences, UW), as reported in Supplementary Table 4. At least three proteotypic peptides and three transitions for each deriving parent ion were selected for the monitoring and quantification. Xevo-TQS triple-quad mass spectrometer, coupled to a nanoAcquity UHPLC (Waters, Milford, MA, United States) equipped with iKey CHIP interface (BEH C18 130 Å, 1.7 μm 150 μm x 50 mm iKey) was used. The LC gradient was set by mixing solvent A (5% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid in water LC-MS grade) and B (5% water LC-MS grade, 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile LC-MS grade) starting from 3 to 35% of B in 7 min and then from 35 to 50% of B in 5 min at 3 µl/min flow rate. Each run was analyzed in duplicate, and the total area of each peptide transition was used for the relative quantification of the specific protein, using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) protein as normalizer.

RESULTS:
Results
Fishing and identification of CPG-tel46 interactome
An affinity purification – mass spectrometry (AP-MS) experiment was set up to extract and identify the proteins specifically interacting with a model of the human telomeric DNA, that is the dimeric G4 structure tel46, covalently bound to our ad hoc synthesized CPG support, from the nuclear lysate of MCF7 cells, selected as model of cancer cells, and specifically of human breast adenocarcinoma. By discarding the proteins identified in the pulldown experiment with the CPG support functionalized with the control duplex oligonucleotide ds27, we identified 112 potential tel46 interactors. The list was further filtered by Crapome database by removing proteins that were found up to 40% of the time in other similar experimental settings reported in the database, and, therefore, considered very frequent contaminants. The final list of potential tel46 binders was reduced to 93 proteins (Supplementary Table 1). The latter ones were functionally analyzed by the Panther platform, interrogating Gene Ontologies classes, such as cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. In the dot plot in Fig. 1, the significant enriched terms for each ontology are represented for Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC) and Molecular Functions (MF).
Among the enriched Biological Processes, we found molecular functions related to G4 structures, such as DNA replication and genome integrity maintenance (i.e., mismatch repair, double-strand break repair via homologous recombination). In particular, the AP experiment selected proteins located at the nuclear replication forks (RF) (fold enrichment close to 50 in Cellular Component analysis) and showing helicase activity (fold enrichment around 13 in Molecular Function analysis), that, together with the new DNA synthesis, represents the principal activity at the RF. The presence of replisome complex in the CC enriched terms also suggests the involvement of DNA replication processes, consistent with the telomeric role of the G4 structure. Actually, these structures cause a physical roadblock to DNA replication, generating a stalled replication fork, which allows the local recruitment of all replication factors required for the process initiation. Moreover, the “mismatch repair” (MMR), the DNA repair mechanism required for proper maintenance of the genome stability by protecting against base pair mismatches and insertion/deletion loops arising from DNA polymerase errors or during homologous recombination was also well represented in the list of Biological Processes. In particular, three proteins were identified as players in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) mechanism: MSH2, MSH6, and RPA3. MSH2 and MSH6 form a heterodimer within the MutSα complex, already described to recognize G4-DNA structures.
Even though unexpected, we also found processes related to translation and mRNA binding, as suggested by cytoplasmic translation initiation in BP, polysome in CC, and translation initiation factor activity in MF terms. In fact, the presence of many subunits of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 3 (eIF3) and proteins reported to bind to mRNA (e.g., LARP4B, ESRP1, CNBP and many others) enriched these classes. Within this group, many proteins showed also nuclear localization. This could be probably explained considering that RNA is also able to fold in G4 structures generally reported to be involved in different processes such as translation regulation, splicing, mRNA localization, as found in the Biological Processes enriched terms.
Analysis of the dysregulated proteins in MCF7 cells
To comprehensively profile the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in breast cancer in comparison to a physiological system, the total proteomes from MCF7 and MCF10A cells were compared by using a label free shotgun approach. The peptide mixtures that were analyzed in triplicate by nanoLC-MS/MS and 3748 proteins were identified and quantified by MaxQuant (Supplementary Table 2). Figure 2 illustrates the results of the differential proteomics analysis comparing the proteomes of the breast cancer cell line (MCF7) and of the non-tumorigenic epithelial cell line (MCF10A). The similarity of the samples was assessed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2A), and Euclidean distances were calculated and displayed in a heatmap (Fig. 2B). The results demonstrated the robustness of the generated mass spectrometry datasets. Indeed, the PCA plot revealed a very high degree of similarity among samples within the same group (MCF7 or MCF10A). Furthermore, as expected, the analysis clearly showed the separation between the proteomes of MCF7 and MCF10A. The two cell types form distinct clusters, with PC1 accounting for most of the variance (57.5%) and PC2 contributing for an additional 8.1%. This strong divergence underscores substantial differences in the overall protein expression profiles, reflecting the different biological processes that play roles in the tumorigenic (MCF7) versus non-tumorigenic (MCF10A) cells. After the Perseus analysis, 2057 statistically significant proteins were retrieved, subsequently refined according to the fold change (FC) constrains. Figure 2B shows a heatmap of statistically significant differentially expressed proteins, where samples are clustered based on Euclidean distances, and proteins are visualized in terms of their relative expression. Analogously to the PCA analysis, the heatmap colour gradient, ranging from blue (down-regulation) to red (up-regulation), reveals clear stratification between the two cell types. The clustering indicates that MCF7 and MCF10A samples exhibit distinct proteomic signatures, with groups of proteins showing markedly different expression levels, further highlighting the divergence between the cancerous and non-tumorigenic states. The analysis yielded the identification and quantification of up- and down-regulated proteins, showing |log₂FC| >0.5 (0.7 and 1.4 on a linear scale for the down- and up-regulated proteins, respectively) graphically represented in a volcano plot (Fig. 2C).
The x-axis represents the log2(Fold Change), indicating the direction and magnitude of change, while the y-axis shows the -log10(FDR), reflecting statistical significance. Notably, 851 proteins are significantly upregulated in MCF7 (depicted as red points), while 639 proteins are significantly downregulated (blue points). The remaining proteins do not meet the thresholds (|log2(Fold Change) | >0.5 and FDR < 1%) and are depicted in grey. The proteins upregulated in MCF7 may be involved in cancer-associated processes such as enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metabolic rewiring, whereas those downregulated may correspond to pathways more active in normal epithelial cells, like differentiation and homeostatic regulation. The results provide valuable insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying breast cancer and may help to identify potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
The functional enrichment analysis derived from the differential proteomics approach is showcased in Fig. 3, revealing the biological roles of proteins that are either upregulated or downregulated in the studied conditions. This analysis offers valuable insight into the distinct cellular pathways and processes affected.
Most of the mechanisms over-represented in the up-regulated proteins dataset (Fig. 3A) are related to DNA repair and replication. For instance, terms such as DNA strand elongation involved in DNA replication, double strand break repair via break-induced replication, spliceosomal snRNP assembly underscore the activation of genome stability mechanisms, likely reflect the cells response to stress or damage. The cellular component output analysis revealed a massive presence of splicing-related complexes among the overexpressed proteins (U1 snRNP, U2 snRNP, U5 snRNP, U4/U6 x U5 tri-snRNP complex), as well as proteins resident in the endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi transport (ER-Golgi intermediate compartment, ER to Golgi transport vesicle membrane). The dysregulation of the splicing machinery is associated with the progression of BC, as reviewed by Read and Natrajan, and aberrant splicing events correspond to the onset and aggression of BC. Similarly, the regulation of ER-to-Golgi trafficking in the context of BC has been addressed in several studies, suggesting an up-regulation of this pathway, which is often associated with cancer progression and metastasis.
On the other hand, Panel B reveals a contrasting profile for the downregulated proteins. These proteins are predominantly associated with fundamental cellular processes, such as cytoplasmic translation, ribosomal subunit biogenesis, and amino acid biosynthesis, pointing to a suppression of protein synthesis machinery. This is further supported by the enrichment of ribosomal components and activities such as rRNA binding and structural molecule activity, suggesting a downregulation of translational capacity. Deregulation of ribosomal protein expression and translation was found to promote BC metastasis, highlighting the role of specific ribosomal proteins in enhancing the metastatic potential of breast cancer cells and suggesting that their down-regulation could be part of the complex response to cancer progression. Additionally, processes related to actin filament bundle assembly and cell-substrate junctions indicated reduced cytoskeletal organization and adhesion, reported in BC by several studies.
Overall, the high overlapping degree of the processes enriched in the interactomics data of MCF7 nuclear extract (Fig. 1) with those found in the up-regulated MCF7 protein dataset (Fig. 3A) would suggest tel46 as a useful tool for biomarker recognition in BC.
Selection of potential breast cancer biomarkers recognized by tel46
To find possible biomarkers recognizing tel46 in BC, we integrated the proteomics analysis, combining interactomics and differential proteomics results, to identify potential biomarkers associated with tel46 interactors and proteins upregulated in MCF7 cells. The approach aimed to narrow down a set of proteins with potential clinical relevance by focusing on those that are both directly associated with tel46 and show significant upregulation in cancer-related pathways. In Fig. 4A, a Venn diagram visualizes the overlap between two datasets: the tel46 interactors identified through affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS) and the proteins significantly up-regulated in MCF7 cells, as determined by differential proteomics. From the 851 proteins over-expressed in MCF7 cells and the 93 tel46 interactors, 27 proteins were found to overlap. This overlay represents a critical subset of proteins that may play significant roles in MCF7 biology, as they are not only upregulated but also likely directly interact with tel46, making them strong candidates for further analyses.
The latter data were compared with the protein expression levels in BC reported in the UALCAN database, which provides statistical evidence of their relevance in BC biology. UALCAN is a user-friendly online platform that provides access to cancer omics data, offering tools for the analysis and visualization of gene expression, mutations, and survival data across various cancer types. It integrates data from large-scale projects like The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and allows users to explore gene expression profiles, clinical data, and tumour-specific variations. We considered as possible biomarkers spotted by tel46 only those proteins that were reported to accumulate in BC with p-value < 1E-04 in UALCAN, obtaining the table displayed in Fig. 4B. The analysis retrieved a refined set of 19 biomarker candidates. The table lists key information for each protein, including the UniProt code for identification, the gene name, the fold change (indicating the degree of up-regulation in MCF7 cells), and the p-value from the UALCAN analysis (highlighting statistical significance). This curated list represents a focused group of proteins with both strong biological relevance and potential functional significance, making them promising targets for further validation as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
The list of 19 proteins which mainly comprises molecules involved in DNA metabolic processes was analyzed to find their co-expression patterns in breast cancer. The results are shown in the correlation matrix in Fig. 4C, obtained by using the RNA-seq data available on Breast Cancer Gene-Expression Miner v5.0 (bc-GenExMiner v5.0) platform, an online tool designed to facilitate the analysis of gene expression data in BC. It allows users to explore the expression levels of genes across different BC subtypes, clinical parameters, and patient survival data. The platform integrates data from public BC datasets, such as TCGA and METABRIC, enabling users to perform in-depth analyses of gene expression, identify potential biomarkers, and explore their clinical significance. The analysis was performed by considering the sample estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors status, calculating Pearson’s correlation indices for each ER and PR combination. Since MCF7 is a cell model known to be positive for ER and PR status, the correlation matrix displayed in Fig. 4C specifically pertains to this context. The diagonal elements represent individual genes, while the lower half of the matrix shows the Pearson correlation coefficients for each pairwise comparison, color-coded from blue (negative correlation) to red (positive correlation). The upper half provides the scatterplots that visualize the relationships between the expression levels of each protein pair.
Notably, almost all comparisons show a positive correlation between expressions, albeit to varying degrees. Many proteins show strong positive correlations, suggesting that they may be co-regulated or involved in similar biological pathways. Conversely, weaker or negative correlations may indicate functional divergence or opposing roles within cellular processes. This result suggests a consistent expression trend among these genes in BC, further supported by proteomic data indicating that all 19 proteins are up regulated in MCF7 cells.
Taken together, these results allowed the integration of interactomics and proteomics obtaining a list of 27 potential BC biomarkers candidates, further refined to a set of 19 through the UALCAN database. The expression levels of these 19 candidates were found to be positively correlated in BC through the analysis performed with bc-GenExMiner platform.
Transcriptional levels of tel46 interactors in BC tissues
To evaluate the potentiality of tel46 as BC biomarkers detector, we investigated the connection of the 19 identified proteins with BC through bioinformatic tools comprising transcriptomics and proteomics data, with the aim of reaching a molecular signature, possibly made by functional interconnecting protein network.
Firstly, we compared the transcript levels of the 19 proteins in breast cancer invasive carcinoma (BRCA) and normal tissues with Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA), an interactive tool that provides interactive analysis of gene expression data, primarily focusing on cancer genomics. By examining the expression profiles of specific transcripts, the analysis identifies genes that are significantly overexpressed in BRCA samples compared to normal tissues, shedding light on potential molecular drivers of BC. In Fig. 5, we reported profile (panel A) and box (panel B) plot of those proteins that were accumulated in the BRCA dataset compared to normal cells with log2FC > 0.5 and p-value < 0.01, as for the proteomics analysis. In Panel A, the expression profiles for 10 over 19 candidates are measured as transcripts per million (TPM) and are plotted for BRCA (red lines) and normal tissues (green lines). The red curves, representing BRCA patients, consistently show higher expression levels compared to the green curves, highlighting the marked up-regulation of these transcripts in cancer tissues. This visual representation underscores a strong association between these transcripts and the tumour phenotype, suggesting that they may play critical roles in processes specific to cancer development or progression. Panel B delves deeper into this comparison with box plots, where the expression levels of each transcript are directly compared between BRCA (red boxes) and normal tissues (grey boxes). For each gene, the y-axis displays the log2-transformed TPM values, and the presence of asterisks indicates statistically significant differences between the two groups.
The data were obtained interrogating The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. In particular, ESRP1, CHD4, LRPPRC, MAGED2, MCM5, MSH2, MSH6, PREX1, PRKCI, WDHD1 mRNA were found enriched in tumour samples compared to normal breast tissues. The 9 remaining proteins were all slightly up-regulated but they did not pass the FC and/or p-value filters (Supplementary Fig. 1).
We subsequently analyzed the mRNA expression profiles of the 19 potential protein biomarkers across various BC subtypes, including basal-like, HER2, luminal A, and luminal B. Figure 6 provides an in-depth analysis of 13 transcripts overexpressed in BRCA tissues compared to normal tissues which identified at least one subtype of the disease stratified across BC subtypes (basal-like, HER2, luminal A, luminal B) using data generated by the GEPIA software. The box plots illustrate the expression levels (log2-transformed TPM + 1) of selected transcripts in each subtype, with red boxes representing BRCA tissues and grey boxes representing normal tissues. The boxplots depicting the expression levels of the 6 remaining genes analyzed are reported in Supplementary Fig. 2. Notably, MSH6, MSH2, CHD4, ESRP1, LRPPRC, and WDHD1 were found to be over-expressed in all cancer subtypes. This finding is promising as it suggests the potential of tel46 as a biomarker detector across a diverse array of BC subtypes. Additionally, MCM5 was upregulated in nearly all subtypes, except in luminal A, where the increase was not statistically significant. Conversely, LARP4B, EIF3B, and DNMT1 showed higher expression primarily in the basal-like subtype. Intriguingly, MAGED2 and PREX1 transcripts were slightly and significantly down-regulated, respectively, in tumour cells of the basal-like subtype compared to their normal counterparts, indicating a possible specificity of these genes for luminal-like cells.
Potential prognostic value of the BC biomarker candidates
To find potential prognostic values for the BC biomarker candidates, the correlation between their transcript levels and clinicopathological and molecular features was interrogated in bc-GenExMiner (v.5.0), a statistical open-source platform applied to published annotated BC transcriptomic data. According to DNA microarrays experiments, for each protein we looked for the age of the tumour onset, nodal status, ER+/-, PR+/-, HER2+/-, mutations in p53 and BRCA1/2 genes, TNBC status, Scarff-Bloom-Richardson grade, and Nottingham prognostic index.
The results, summarized in Table 1, showed that MSH6, MSH2, PRKCI, ESRP1, DNMT1, MCM5, WDHD1, FAM50A, EIF3B, LARP4B, NSUN2 were associated to the highest malignant and poor prognostic BC outcomes in patients group characterized by ER and PR negative status mutations in p53 and BRCA1/2 genes, onset in younger age (≤ 51 years old), nodal metastasis status, level III of Scarff-Bloom-Richardson grade and Nottingham prognostic index. These findings suggest that overexpression of the above-mentioned proteins might have an unfavourable prognostic impact in terms of clinical outcome of BC and might be specifically associated with the TNBC-like phenotype.
Contrarily, MAGED2, PREX1, DDX23, USP8, CHD4, LRPPRC, PSPC1 over-expression could be associated with milder clinical phenotype.
The 19 potential biomarkers recognized by tel46 were also evaluated in terms of survival analysis with Kaplan-Meier plotter tool, designed to analyze and visualize the survival data of patients based on gene expression. It integrates data from multiple cancer types, including breast, ovarian, lung, and gastric cancers, and allows users to generate Kaplan-Meier survival curves to assess the prognostic value of specific genes. In detail, the relapse-free survival (RFS), the distant metastasis -free survival (DMFS), and the overall survival (OS) were probed considering all the ER/PR/HER2 status and all the BC subtypes. The bioinformatic platform calculated the hazard ratio (HR) and the p-value associated with the analysis of each item. HR bigger than 1 indicates the higher probability to a poorer prognostic outcome when the gene is highly expressed. In contrast, when HR is lower than 1, it means that the gene is a marker for good prognosis. We initially analyzed our panel using the KMplot protein-level tool. We observed that, for the many candidate proteins, the hazard ratios (HRs) were consistently > 1, suggesting a negative prognostic value. However, in most cases, p-values remained > 0.05, indicating a likely insufficient statistical power due to small cohort size or missing data for those specific protein targets. Therefore, we switched the analysis at transcriptional levels. In Fig. 7 (panels A, B, and C) we reported the results obtained analyzing the expression levels of the 19 potential biomarkers in terms of RFS, DMFS, and OS, respectively. In particular, the forest plot provides a visual summary of the relationship between gene expression and survival outcomes, represented through hazard ratios (HR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), retrieved by Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis. Each row in tables in Fig. 7 panels A, B, and C corresponds to a specific gene, with HR indicating whether higher expression is associated with better (HR < 1) or worse (HR > 1) survival. In parenthesis the 95% CIs are reported. The graphs visually display the HR values for each gene and negative, positive, and non-significant survival outcome is indicated with red, green, and black coloured dots. We obtained that 10 of the 19 proteins are correlated with poor survival prognosis in at least 2 clinical outcome measures (RFS, DMFS, OS) when their mRNAs are over-expressed. Figure 7, panel D shows the KM-plots of those proteins. This result suggested that the accumulation of MSH6, MSH2, PRKCI, ESRP1, DNMT1, MCM5, WDHD1, FAM50A, EIF3B, NSUN2 is associated with a low patient survival either because of the insurgence of relapses, metastases or death.
Cross-referencing the data from all bioinformatics evaluation, our data suggest that MSH2, MSH6, ESRP1, WDHD1 could be proposed as general prognostic biomarkers in BC recognized by tel46.
Validation of the selected proteins as potential biomarkers
To validate the biological meaningfulness of the selected candidates, we performed an AP-MS experiment starting from non-tumorigenic MCF10A cell line (Supplementary Table 3), using the same protocol previously described for MCF7. Remarkably, none of the previously identified candidate proteins (ESRP1, WDHD1, MSH2, MSH6) were detected in the MCF10A dataset as displayed in the graph in Fig. 8A. The finding strengthens the hypothesis that their binding to telomeric G4 structures occurs exclusively in the tumorigenic context. These results support the cancer-specific relevance of tel46-associated protein interactions and reinforce the biomarker potential of the selected proteins.
In addition, to validate the direct interaction of candidate biomarkers with the telomeric probe tel46, we performed in vitro pulldown assays with the recombinant forms of the selected proteins (WDHD1, ESRP1, MSH2 as representative of the MSH2/MSH6 MutSα complex) and tel46 derivatized CPG support, using naked CPG as control. In Fig. 8B, we reported the Coomassie stained gel. Each selected protein was specifically retained on tel46-derivatised CPG, confirming the direct binding of each candidate to the G4 structure. The theoretical molecular weights were consistent with the observed bands occurring in the reference lanes, where 2 µg of each recombinant protein were loaded as input. These results not only validate AP-MS data but mainly confirm that the interaction between these proteins and tel46 is not mediated by additional nuclear factors. The direct physical recognition of the selected proteins with tel46 strongly support the hypothesis that tel46 can be used as a selective scaffold for the fishing of relevant proteins involved in genome stability and DNA repair. Finally, all four candidates’ expression levels in MCF7 and in MCF10A were validated by measuring their relative quantification through a Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) LC-MSMS approach (see Supplementary Table 4 for quantification details). As shown in the box-and-whisker plot reported in Fig. 8C, for all four biomarkers the overexpression trends in luminal cancer cells in comparison to the heathy controls were confirmed.

INTRODUCTION:
Background
The pursuit of reliable biomarkers is essential for improving early detection, accurate prognosis, and effective treatment of breast cancer (BC), which remains one of the most prevalent cancer forms globally. BC accounted for approximately 11.7% of all new cancer cases worldwide, making it a leading health concern. Biomarkers are pivotal in modern oncology, facilitating early diagnosis, monitoring treatment response, and personalizing therapeutic strategies. Their role is critical for advancing precision medicine and improving survival rates by enabling timely intervention and tailored treatment plans. Among the many molecular targets being explored, human telomeric DNA, consisting of tandem repeats of the sequence TTAGGG and thus able to fold into G-quadruplex (G4) structures, emerged as key player in cancer biology. G4s influence processes like transcription, replication, and genomic stability, which are often altered in cancer. Notably, G4s are enriched in regulatory regions of oncogenes such as MYC and TP53 and are associated with increased transcriptional activity in cancer cells. Furthermore, the telomere 3’-overhang rich in guanines can be the substrate of telomerase, a DNA polymerase, overexpressed in cancer cells, that elongates telomeric DNA thus counteracting telomere shortening and leading to cellular immortality and finally cancer development and progression. This makes telomeres attractive targets for cancer biomarker discovery and therapeutic intervention. In cancer cells, telomeric G4s interact with various nuclear proteins, including those involved in DNA replication, repair, and genome stability. These interactions offer unique opportunities to uncover cancer-specific protein partners and potential biomarkers. Recent advancements in proteomics and bioinformatics have enabled the systematic identification of G4-interacting proteins, revealing their roles in DNA metabolism, splicing regulation, and cell cycle progression These studies have highlighted the potential of G4s not only as therapeutic targets in targeted anticancer strategies but also as platforms for discovering disease biomarkers. Identifying novel biomarkers is fundamental to enable precise patient stratification and deliver personalized treatment based on individual tumour biology. Current BC diagnostics rely predominantly on immunohistochemical markers such as ER, PR, and HER2, complemented by molecular assays like FISH and multigene panels (e.g., Oncotype DX). Conventional diagnostic tools provide valuable information but often fall short in revealing functional protein-level changes. Affinity Purification–Mass Spectrometry (AP‑MS) is increasingly recognized as a powerful method for biomarker discovery, capable of mapping protein complexes and specific interactions of G4 structures. Moreover, MS-based proteomics workflows, transitioning from discovery to targeted assays (e.g., MRM), are well-suited for developing clinically applicable tests, including detection of G4-bound protein biomarkers in enriched samples.
The study of the proteins interacting with telomeric G4s can be considered a crucial aspect to elucidate the biological mechanisms in which these DNA structures could be involved, especially in cancer cells. Several proteins, besides telomerase, have been reported to interact with telomeric overhang DNA and exert various biological functions. A chemoproteomic-driven approach was previously applied to the identification of the nuclear proteins from the HCT116 cancer cells interacting with the 26-mer truncation of the human telomeric DNA sequence d[(TTAGGG)4TT] (tel26) structured in a monomeric G-quadruplex. In this study three novel proteins (i.e., HMGB1, KHSRP, and LMNB1) that specifically bind to the monomeric G4 model of the human telomeric DNA were discovered, starting from a nuclear extract of human colon carcinoma cell line. The identification of these proteins suggests that G4 structures are more than just inhibitory elements for telomerase activity. In contrast, they may act as critical regulatory hubs at the telomeres, providing a scaffold for proteins that influence telomere maintenance, stability, and function. These results emphasize the biological significance of G4 structures, highlighting their role in maintaining genome integrity and their potential as therapeutic targets. Particularly in cancer, where telomere dysfunction is a hallmark, understanding how G4s interact with specific proteins could lead to innovative strategies for targeting telomeric pathways.
Similarly, one of the most exciting applications of G4s is their integration into biosensors for specific pathology diagnosis and prognosis. G4-based-aptasensors, as they are often called, utilize the binding capabilities of G4 forming aptamers (i.e., short natural or modified oligonucleotide strands that can fold into stable G4 structures) towards specific targets (proteins, cell-receptors, metabolites, etc.). For instance, in cancer diagnostics, G4-based aptasensors have been used to detect crucial biomarkers such as the circulating proteins thrombin and VEGF, or the membrane proteins HER2 (epidermal growth factor receptor 2) and MUC1 (mucin 1). These biosensors are often paired with advanced detection techniques like fluorescence, electrochemistry, and chemiluminescence, significantly enhancing their sensitivity and making them capable of detecting targets at extremely low concentrations. G4s represents a game-changer compared to traditional diagnostic tools. For example, unlike antibodies, which are very sensitive to changes in temperature or pH, G4 structures remain robust across various conditions, ensuring consistent performance. They are also cost-effective; being chemically synthesized, G4-based aptamers are far less expensive to produce than antibodies. Another advantage is their reusability. Indeed, unlike traditional diagnostic tools, G4-based aptamers can often be regenerated and used multiple times, adding an element of sustainability to their application.
Since the 3′-end of the human telomeric DNA is 100–200 bases long and can form higher-order structures by clustering several consecutive G4 units (multimers), here, we undertook a proteomic study choosing a model of the telomeric overhang able to form two consecutive G4 structures, linked to a Controlled Pore Glass (CPG) support ad hoc synthesized for fishing applications. Indeed, we investigated the nuclear interactome of the telomeric dimeric G4 model of sequence d[AGGG(TTAGGG)7] (tel46). Using AP-MS, we identified proteins selectively binding to the dimeric G4 structures in MCF7 breast cancer cells. The latter, which are estrogen receptor-positive and representative of the luminal A BC subtype, were chosen due to their well-characterized and clinically relevant molecular profile. The AP-MS approach revealed 93 tel46-binding proteins, many of which are involved in DNA replication, repair, and genome stability, processes frequently disrupted in cancer. By integrating AP-MS with quantitative proteomics comparing MCF7 to non-tumorigenic MCF10A cells, we identified a subset of 27 interactors that were significantly upregulated in tumour cells. As a non-tumorigenic counterpart, we included MCF10A cells, an immortalized epithelial line, to serve as a baseline reference for physiological nuclear protein composition either in the differential proteomics and interactomics approaches. Further bioinformatic validation across public cancer proteomics and transcriptomics databases supported 19 candidates, among which MSH2, MSH6, ESRP1, and WDHD1 emerged as the most promising potential biomarkers. To explore whether the proteins captured in MCF7 reflect a broader BC signature, we extended our analyses using publicly available proteomic and transcriptomic databases across multiple BC subtypes. This approach enabled us to assess the diagnostic and prognostic potential of the tel46-binding protein panel beyond the luminal context, laying the groundwork for subtype-spanning biomarker discovery.
This preliminary work paves the way to tel46 or other cancer-related sequences as tools for biomarker discovery and highlights its application in predicting molecular signatures associated with breast cancer subtypes. Finally, we propose MSH2, MSH6, ESRP1, WDHD1 proteins as candidate biomarkers recognized by tel46 which could be possibly used in potential diagnostic and prognostic applications for BC.
