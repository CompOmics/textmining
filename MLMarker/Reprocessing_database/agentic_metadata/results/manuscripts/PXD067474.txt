ABSTRACT:
Lyme neuroborreliosis (LNB) is a nervous system infection caused by tick-borne spirochetes of the Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato complex and is one of the most frequent bacterial infections of the nervous system in Europe. Early diagnosis remains challenging due to limited sensitivity and specificity of current tests and the need for invasive lumbar punctures, highlighting the need for improved and potentially less invasive diagnostic tools.  In this study, we used bottom-up proteomics to analyze 308 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples and 207 plasma samples from well-characterized cohorts, including patients with LNB, viral meningitis, other manifestations of Lyme borreliosis without central nervous system involvement, and controls with normal CSF findings. We identified diagnostic panels of regulated proteins and evaluated their performance using machine learning–based classifiers.  The resulting models distinguished LNB from viral meningitis and controls in CSF with areas under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.92 and 0.90, respectively. In plasma, LNB was distinguished from controls with an AUC of 0.80. These findings suggest a potential diagnostic role for proteomics-based biomarker panels in LNB and provide a large-scale, publicly available proteomic resource for further biomarker discovery and validation studies. SAMPLE PROCESSING:
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma samples were thawed and aliquoted into 96-well plates. For CSF, 20 µL of sample was mixed with 30 µL PreOmics lysis buffer and denatured at room temperature for 10 minutes. For plasma, 5 µL of sample was mixed with 45 µL PreOmics lysis buffer and denatured under the same conditions. Proteins were digested for 4 hours using a LysC/trypsin enzyme mix. Peptides were purified using two-gauge SDB-RPS StageTips (∼20 minutes processing time) and eluted for LC–MS/MS analysis.  Peptide separation was performed on an Evosep One (Evosep Biosystems, Denmark) liquid chromatography system using the 60 samples-per-day gradient and an 8 cm PepSep column. The LC system was coupled online to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating in positive ion mode with data-independent acquisition (DIA). Fragmentation was performed by higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 30%. Spectra were acquired at a resolution of 30,000 (Orbitrap) with a maximum injection time of 28 ms.

DATA PROCESSING:
Raw DIA mass spectrometry data were processed with DIA-NN (version 1.9) using a data-independent search strategy and a human spectral library. Peptide and protein quantifications were exported for further analysis.  Data curation and statistical analysis were performed using the Clinical Knowledge Graph (CKG), AlphapeptStats, and Jupyter Notebook environments. Quality control included removal of samples with low protein counts (below 1.5× IQR from the 25th percentile of the combined distribution) and exclusion of proteins with >40% missing values across samples. Protein intensities were log₂-transformed. Remaining missing values were imputed using a variational autoencoder implemented in the PIMMS software.  Batch effects were corrected using ComBat to account for potential plate-specific biases. Differential abundance analysis was performed with unpaired t-tests and Benjamini–Hochberg multiple hypothesis correction (adjusted p < 0.05).  For classification model development, significant proteins from the development cohorts were used as input features in supervised machine learning algorithms (scikit-learn library). Model selection and hyperparameter tuning were performed by stratified k-fold cross-validation (5-fold for CSF, 3-fold for plasma) optimized for the weighted F1 score. Model performance was assessed by area under the ROC curve (AUC) and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) in both development and validation cohorts.
