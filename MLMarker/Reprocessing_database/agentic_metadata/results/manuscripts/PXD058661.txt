ABSTRACT:
The data submitted is for the proteomics data published in the paper: A systematic evaluation of isolation techniques and freeze-thaw effects on plasma extracellular vesicle heterogeneity and subpopulation profiling

PROJECT DESCRIPTION:
A systematic evaluation of isolation techniques and freeze-thaw effects on plasma extracellular vesicle heterogeneity and subpopulation profiling

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
EV samples were lysed with lysis buffer (0.5% SDC (w/v), 10 mM TCEP, 40 mM CAA, 50 mM TEAB), sonicated for 10 minutes, then incubated at 95℃ for 10 minutes for protein extraction, denaturation and Alkylation. Alkylated samples were cleaned up using the SP3 method (Nature Protocols, Joseph D. Card et al, 2019). In short, carboxylate beads mix (100 µg/µl in H2O, Sera-Mag Speed beads, cat# 45152105050350 and 65152105050350, GE Healthcare, IL, USA) and ethanol were added to samples (1 µg/µl, diluted with lysis buffer), following by 15 minutes of incubation and three wash steps with 80% ethanol. The beads were resuspended with 100 µl of digestion buffer (50 mM HEPES) and digested with Trypsin (Promega, Catalog # V5117) overnight at 37 ℃ and 1000 rpm using a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein ratio. The peptides were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, 5µg of peptides were acidified by 10% formic acid (FA) to reach a final concentration of 1% and then desalted using Ziptip Pipette Tips (ZTC18S960, Millipore). Samples were dried in SpeedVac and reconstituted in 0.1% FA for LC-MS/MS analysis.

DATA PROCESSING:
The project-specific spectral library was generated using DIA-MS2pep. Protein sequences were retrieved from the UniProt human proteome database (uniport _UP000005640_9606.fasta, 20,588 entries). Trypsin was specified as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Precursor and product ion mass tolerances were set at 20 ppm. Carbamidomethylation of cysteines was a fixed modification, while methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were variable modifications. Peptide lengths were restricted to 7–50 amino acids. FDRs for peptides and proteins were estimated, and the library was processed in DIA-NN (v1.8) with a 1% FDR threshold for protein quantification. Default settings were used for all other parameters.

METHODS:
Materials and Methods
Plasma Collection
Peripheral blood was collected from a fasting, healthy 25‐year‐old male volunteer using EDTA‐coated collection tubes (Yuli, China). The blood was initially centrifuged at 500 g for 15 min at room temperature to separate plasma, which then underwent secondary centrifugation at 2000 g for 15 min to remove cells, large debris and platelets. The collected plasma was aliquoted into cryotubes and stored at −80°C. A subset of plasma samples was stored directly at −80°C without any freeze‐thaw handling, while another set underwent freeze‐thaw handling at an interval of 3–7 days. For freeze‐thaw handling, plasma samples were thawed at room temperature for 30 min and then returned to −80°C storage. For all subsequent EV isolation experiments, we consistently used 500 µL of plasma as the starting material.
Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of Guangzhou National Laboratory (No. 2024‐0007), Guangzhou, China, and the study followed the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from the volunteer.
EV Isolation Using AF4 Technology
The EVs‐containing pellets were derived by diluting 500 µL of plasma samples to a volume of 2.4 mL with PBS, followed by a centrifugation process at 120,000 g for 1.5 h at a temperature of 4°C (Sorvall WX100+ centrifuge, Thermo Scientific). Subsequent to centrifugation, pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of degassed PBS served as both the equilibration and elution buffer within the isolation system. This suspension was then introduced into the AF4‐MALS system (Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, USA), situated downstream of an Agilent quaternary pump and autosampler (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA). 152 mm isolation channel fitted with a 10 kDa regenerated cellulose membrane (RC10) was applied. Flow rates and parameters used in the study are detailed in the Table S1. Four distinct peaks were discernible during the process, with EVs being predominantly located in the P4 fraction. These fractions were then transferred to a 50 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Merck Millipore) and concentrated by centrifugation at 3000 g at 4°C down to a final volume of 100 µL for subsequent analysis. Data collection and analysis were conducted using ASTRA software (Version 6.1, Wyatt Technology, Santa Barbara, USA).
EV Isolation Using Exo‐CMDS Technology
Two buffer solutions, ExoL and ExoI, were employed for the Exo‐CMDS method (Liang et al.). ExoL, prepared with 100 mM 1,3‐Bis(tris(hydroxymethyl)methylamino) propane (ThermoFisher, USA), 150 mM sodium chloride (Macklin, China) and 2.7 mM potassium chloride (Macklin, China) at pH 6.5, served as the loading buffer. The isolating buffer ExoI was composed of the same molarity of 1,3‐Bis(tris(hydroxymethyl)methylamino) propane and potassium chloride, but with an increased sodium chloride concentration of 1000 mM and 0.05% Tween‐20 (Yeasen, China), adjusted to pH 6.4. The plasma samples, along with ExoL and ExoI, were introduced into their respective loading chambers.
A 3000 rpm centrifugation for 22 s filtered 500 µL of plasma through membrane 1, a filtration membrane, to remove contaminants. The filtrations were blended with the ExoL outflow from the quantitative chamber, while ExoI entered the first stage buffer chamber. The workstation executed three cycles at biphasic speeds—3000 rpm clockwise for 8 s followed by 1000 rpm in reverse for 8 s—to ensure thorough mixing. Following this, the plasma‐ExoL mixture was propelled through membrane 2 at 3000 rpm anticlockwise for 50 s to adhere EVs onto the membrane, discarding the flow‐through into the waste chamber and ExoI advanced into the second‐stage buffer chamber. Final centrifugation at 3000 rpm clockwise for 50 s enabled the elution of the EVs with ExoI into the collection chamber. The isolated EVs were then stored at 4°C for further use. Prior to subsequent detection, desalination of the EVs solution was performed using a 10 kDa cutoff filter, with the conditions set at 3000 g and 4°C for 30 min.
EV Isolation Using SEC Technology
The resins Sepharose CL‐4B (Sigma, USA) were washed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). The washed resin was then poured into an Econo‐Pac Chromatography Column (Bio‐Rad, USA) to achieve a bed volume of 3 mL. Once the desired amount of resin filled the column and the liquid had dripped through, the top frit was promptly placed at the resin's top.
For SEC method, 500 µL plasma samples were initially centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 30 min. The supernatant was then carefully added to the top of the SEC column. The EVs containing fractions were collected in a tube. The collected fractions were subsequently concentrated using 100 K Amicon Ultra‐15 centrifugal filter units (EMD Millipore, USA).
NTA
The size and concentration of the isolated particles were measured using a NanoSight NS300 platform equipped with a 405 nm laser and an sCMOS camera from Malvern Instruments (UK). The NTA 3.3 software package was used for analysis. For each sample, three consecutive videos were recorded at room temperature, and a syringe pump (speed 50) was used for sample injection. For each isolation method, the total particle yield per sample was estimated by multiplying the NTA concentration by the elution volume, and normalized to the initial plasma input (500 µL).
TEM
To examine plasma EV morphology, 10 µL of EVs was incubated for 5 min on a formvar film‐coated FF200‐Cu grid (200 mesh, CU). Negative staining was then performed with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 1 min. Images were obtained under a HITACHI HT7800 Transmission Electron Microscope (Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).
Single‐EV Surface Proteome Profiling by PBA
Isolated EVs were centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. To capture the exosomes, a 96‐well plate was prepared with cholera toxin subunit B (CTB, abs80001, Absin, China) at 2 µg/mL in coating buffer (CB01100, Thermofisher Scientific, USA) which was incubated overnight at 4°C, then washed thrice with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST). One microlitre of each sample was diluted with 19 µL of PBA buffer and added to each well with CTB coating to enable exosome affinity capture via CTB‐GM1 lipid membrane interactions. A panel of 262 antibodies, each labelled with oligonucleotides comprising distinct protein identifiers, molecular tags and a universal primer‐binding sequence, was employed. This setup facilitated the detection of biomarkers at an individual exosome level using PBA. To each well with captured exosomes, 1 µL of the antibody mixture (each antibody at a concentration of 2 µg/mL) was added. The plate was incubated for 2 h at room temperature before plate washing to remove excess antibodies. Oligonucleotide tags specific to each exosome were incorporated during the extension reaction using exosome tag templates. Indexing and sequencing adapters were integrated using specially designed primers during PCR. Sequencing was executed DNBSEQ‐T7 platform (MGI, Shenzhen, China) using a PE150 sequencing kit. The output was generated as a bcl file for each sample transferred into fastq files with aid of bioinformatic tools MegaBolt (MGI, Shenzhen, China). PBA reagent kits were provided by Secretech (Shanghai, China), adhering to the standard operating procedures developed in earlier study (Wu et al.).
EV Proteome Profiling Using LC‐MS/MS
EV samples were lysed with lysis buffer (0.5% SDC (w/v), 10 mM TCEP, 40 mM CAA, 50 mM TEAB), sonicated for 10 min, then incubated at 95°C for 10 min for protein extraction, denaturation and Alkylation. Alkylated samples were cleaned up using the SP3 method (Hughes et al.,). In short, carboxylate beads mix (100 µg/µL in H2O, Sera‐Mag Speed beads, cat# 45152105050350 and 65152105050350, GE Healthcare, IL, USA) and ethanol were added to samples (1 µg/µL, diluted with lysis buffer), following by 15 min of incubation and three wash steps with 80% ethanol. The beads were resuspended with 100 µL of digestion buffer (50 mM HEPES) and digested with Trypsin (Promega, Catalog # V5117) overnight at 37 °C and 1000 rpm using a 1:50 trypsin‐to‐protein ratio. The peptides were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, 5 µg of peptides were acidified by 10% formic acid (FA) to reach a final concentration of 1% and then desalted using Ziptip Pipette Tips (ZTC18S960, Millipore). Samples were dried in SpeedVac and reconstituted in 0.1% FA for LC‐MS/MS analysis.
All nanoLC‐MS/MS experiments were performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a Vanquish Neo_UPLC system (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were loaded onto a 300 um id × 5 mm trap column (Acclaim PepMap NEO C18, Thermo Scientific), then isolated onto a homemade reversed‐phase C18 analytical column (75 um id × 17 cm). The peptides bounded on the column were eluted with a 120‐min linear gradient. The solvent A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water solution and the solvent B consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. The segmented gradient was 5.8% B, 1.8 min; 5.8%–6.3% B, 0.2 min; 6.3%–23% B, 74 min; 23%–36% B, 35 min; 36%–55% B, 1.5 min; 55%–99% B, 0.5 min; 99% B, 7 min at a constant flow rate of 400 nL/min.
The MS analysis was performed with Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). With the data‐independent acquisition mode, the MS scans were acquired at a high resolution of 120,000 (m/z 200) across the mass range of 400–1100 m/z. The AGC target value was 3.00E + 06 with a maximum injection time of 22 ms. The MS/MS scans were acquired with 40 variable overlapping precursor isolation windows and centred at: 410, 427.5, 442.5, 455.5, 467, 478, 488.5, 498, 507.5, 517.5, 527.5, 537, 546.5, 556.5, 566.5, 576, 585.5, 595.5, 606.5, 617.5, 627.5, 638.5, 650, 661, 672, 683.5, 696, 709.5, 724, 740, 757.5, 775.5, 794, 813.5, 837.5, 868.5, 907, 953.5, 1009 and 1089.5. Programmed normalized collision energy was set to 25%, 27%, 30%. The AGC target value was 1.00E + 06 with a maximum injection time of 40 ms in MS2. For nano electro‐spray ion source setting, the spray voltage was 2.1 kV; no sheath gas flow; the heated capillary temperature was 320°C.
The project‐specific MS spectral library was first generated by DIA‐MS2pep as previously described (Hou et al.). Protein sequences for database search were reviewed Human proteome (uniport _UP000005640_9606.fasta, 20588 entries). Trypsin (full) was specified as proteolytic enzyme allowing up to two missed cleavages. The mass tolerance for both precursor and product ions were set to 20 ppm. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as fixed modification, oxidation on Met and acetylation on protein N‐term were specified as variable modifications. The range of peptide length was set from 7 to 50. The run‐specific FDR of identifications at both peptide and protein levels were estimated by Percolator (Spivak et al.). The spectral library was further generated and submitted to DIA‐NN (v1.8) software (Demichev et al.) for protein quantification. Precursor and protein FDR were set to 1%. All the other parameters in DIA‐NN were set to default values.
Analysis of PBA‐Based Single‐EV Surface Proteome Profiling
The sequencing data of PBA test was decoded into single EV proteomic data with aid of EVisualizer miEV software version 1.0.0 (Secretech, Shanghai, China). In total, protein counts for 262 EV membrane proteins were obtained across hundreds of thousands EVs, enabling a detailed single‐EV‐level protein expression profiling analysis. Bulk‐level surface protein expression profiles were generated by summing the counts of each protein across all EVs in each sample (Table S2), followed by normalization to the total protein expression per sample to eliminate the bias caused by EV concentration differences. Trimmed Mean of M‐values (TMM) normalization was then performed using the edgeR R package (Robinson and Oshlack). Differential surface protein expression (DEPs) analysis on the bulk proteome was conducted using multifactor ANOVA. Proteins with a log2FC greater than zero and False Discovery Rate (FDR) less than 0.05 were classified as significantly upregulated DEPs, while those with a log2FC less than zero and FDR less than 0.05 were classified as significantly downregulated DEPs.
Clustering analysis was performed for only EVs with at least two proteins expressed based on the single‐EV‐level protein expression profiles measured by PBA. The Seurat (V5.0) (Hao et al.) package in R was used for the analysis, starting with normalization using the ‘LogNormalize’ function (scale factor of 1e+07), followed by data compression with the LeverageScore algorithm into a sketch matrix. The ‘ProjectData’ function was employed to extend clustering results to the entire dataset. We used the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function in Seurat package to identify the potential marker proteins of a given EV subpopulation and the proteins that had an FDR value smaller than 0.05 and were expressed in at least 20% of the EVs in this subpopulation were denoted as the final marker proteins. FDR values were calculated by the Benjamini–Hochberg method.
Analysis of LC‐MS/MS‐Based EV Proteome Profiling
A total of 1588 proteins have been identified using LC‐MS/MS‐based bulk EV proteome profiling (Table S4). Protein expression levels were quantified based on the intensity value from proteins with at least two unique peptides. The protein expression profiles were normalized using log2 transformation. Proteins present in at least 60% of samples were selected for downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis was conducted using R package limma (Ritchie et al.). Proteins with p values < 0.05 and log2FC > zero were identified as upregulated DEPs, while those with p values < 0.05 and log2FC < zero were classified as downregulated DEPs. Clustering analysis was performed based on the log2 transformed protein expression profiles, using ‘Ward.D2’ method.
Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.3.1). ANOVA test was applied for experiments comparing more than two groups, with FDR values calculated by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. An FDR less than 0.05 was considered statistical significance. For comparisons involving only two groups, the Wilcoxon test was employed. Statistical significance for p values was defined as less than 0.05.
Functional annotations of proteins were extracted from the EBI Gene Ontology Annotation Database (https://geneontology.org/, released date: 2023‐11‐15). GO terms with experimental evidence codes, traceable author statements (TAS) or curators‐inferred statements (IC) were retained for functional analysis. Term definitions and semantic relationships were sourced from the OBO file published on 2023‐11‐15, with obsolete terms excluded from the annotation process. Go enrichment analysis of DEPs was performed using the R package ‘clusterProfiler’. Go terms with p values less than 0.05 and enrichment of more than five proteins were considered significantly enriched.
The lists of tissue and single cell‐enriched genes were downloaded from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org/, version 23.0, Table S5) (Karlsson et al.; Uhlén et al.). A hypergeometric test was applied to assess whether a specific gene set was significantly enriched in particular tissues or cell types. p values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS:
Results
The study design is illustrated in Figure 1. EVs were isolated from stored plasma samples using three different technologies (AF4, Exo‐CMDS and SEC), with samples processed both with and without freeze‐thaw handling. The operational characteristics of these three EV isolation methods are listed in Table S3. For comprehensive characterization of the isolated EVs, we employed multiple complementary analytical approaches: TEM was used to examine the morphology of the isolated particles, NTA to determine the particle size distribution and concentration, PBA for single‐EV surface proteome profiling and LC‐MS/MS to assess the bulk proteome.
Morphology and Size Distribution of Isolated Particles
EVs isolated using AF4, Exo‐CMDS and SEC were confirmed by TEM, showing the typical cup‐shaped or spherical EV morphology (Figure 2a). NTA was utilized to assess the concentration and size distribution of the isolated particles. The results showed that the yields of EVs isolated by SEC were significantly higher, followed by those isolated by Exo‐CMDS, while the AF4 samples exhibited the lowest particle yields (Figure 2b, FDR < 0.05, Table S6). EVs isolated by AF4 showed a relatively narrow size distribution ranging from 50 to 130 nm, while those isolated using SEC displayed an intermediate size distribution of 50–160 nm (Figure 2c). In contrast, EVs isolated by Exo‐CMDS exhibited the widest size distribution, ranging from 50 to 190 nm, suggesting that Exo‐CMDS might isolate a greater proportion of larger EVs compared to other methods. No significant differences in particle concentration were observed between samples with or without freeze‐thaw handling across all three EV isolation methods, indicating that a single freeze‐thaw cycle had minimal effect on EV yield.
PBA‐Based Single‐EV Surface Proteome Profiling
To systematically investigate the composition of EV populations, we employed the PBA‐based single‐EV surface proteome profiling method to analyse EVs isolated using AF4, Exo‐CMDS and SEC from 18 plasma samples under both freeze‐thaw and non‐freeze‐thaw conditions. Additionally, six original plasma samples, treated with or without freeze‐thaw cycles, were included as controls for PBA measurements. To ensure reproducibility and statistical robustness, we set three technical replicates for each of the 24 samples in the PBA measurement. In total, 262 membrane proteins were analysed for EVs in each of the samples. These proteins included classical EV markers such as CD9, CD63 and CD81, alongside other markers such as integrins (e.g., ITGA4, ITGB1), immune cell markers (e.g., CD3E, CD4, CD19, CD20, CD14, CD68) and disease‐related markers (e.g., EGFR, CD44, CEACAM5, PECAM1) (Wu et al.) (see full list in Table S6).
For each sample, bulk‐level protein expression profiles were derived by aggregating individual protein expression values across all detected EVs (See methods). The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plot showed a clear separation of the EV samples isolated by different technologies, indicating notable differences in EV compositions (Figure 3a). The EV samples from the Exo‐CMDS group showed the highest consistency in surface protein profiles, with the highest average sample‐wise Spearman correlation coefficients among the isolation methods (Figure 3a–c). Notably, we observed the largest variation in EV surface protein expression in the plasma‐control samples, which did not undergo any EV isolation procedure (Figure 3c).
In all technology groups, the majority of EVs were detected with one single protein surface marker, followed by those with two proteins detected, and then three or more (Figure 3d, Table S8). We further analysed the proportion of EVs positive for the classical EV markers CD9, CD63 and CD81 in the PBA analysis. These three proteins were consistently detected across all isolation methods. The percentage of CD9+ and CD81+ EVs was highest in the AF4 samples and lowest in the Plasma control samples (FDR < 0.05, Figure 3e, Table S9). In general, the percentage of CD63+ EVs was low in all three isolation methods and plasma control samples, with less than 0.2% (Figure 3e). Additionally, we evaluated the proportion of EV‐related markers reported by the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles 2023 (MISEV2023) (Welsh et al.) (Table S10, Figure S1). The AF4 group exhibited the highest proportions of EVs expressing markers such as ITGA4, ITGB1, ADAM10 and LAMP1 (FDR < 0.05, Figure 3e, Table S9). In contrast, the Exo‐CMDS samples showed the highest proportions of integrins such as integrin alpha (ITGA) and integrin beta (ITGB) families, and heparan sulphate proteoglycans such as SDC1. The Plasma‐control samples displayed the highest percentage of ITGA1+ EVs, followed by the SEC samples ( < 0.05, Table S9, Figure 3e).
Variations in EV Subpopulations Across Different Isolation Methods
Next, we investigated the technology‐specific EV subpopulations by examining EVs detected with either a single surface protein or multiple surface proteins. For EVs with a single surface protein detected, we performed an ANOVA test to compare their prevalence across all groups. This analysis identified 35 single‐protein EVs enriched in the AF4 group, 229 in Exo‐CMDS, 42 in SEC and 28 in the Plasma‐control group (FDR < 0.05, Figure 4a, Table S11). Among these single‐protein EVs, 17 were highest enriched in AF4, 167 in Exo‐CMDS, two in SEC and five in the plasma‐control group. Interestingly, we observed several tetraspanin protein positive EVs, such as CD151+, CD81+ and CD9+, were highly enriched in the AF4 group. In contrast, the Exo‐CMDS group enriched EVs expressing CD5, a classical surface receptor associated with lymphocytes (Veletic et al.), as well as several myeloid‐derived cell surface markers such as Ly6C and Ly6G (Knier et al.). In addition, the Exo‐CMDS group showed enrichment of EVs carrying integrins. In both the SEC and plasma‐control groups, relatively few unique single‐protein EVs were identified, with ITGA1+ being the most significantly enriched in the plasma‐control group, followed by SEC.
For EVs with multiple detected surface proteins, we identified an average of 371,687 EVs in the AF4 group, 255,186 in Exo‐CMDS group, 296,864 in SEC group and 288,660 in plasma‐control group. To evaluate the similarities of these EV populations, we performed unsupervised clustering based on single‐EV‐level protein expression profiles. This analysis revealed 15 distinct EV subpopulations (PBA_c0‐c14), each of which showed a distinct distribution across different isolation methods (Figure 4b,c). To identify technology‐specific EV subpopulations, we compared the proportions of each EV subpopulation across different technology groups (Figures S2 and 4d) and defined an EV subpopulation as technology‐specific if its average proportion was significantly higher in one group than in the others. The marker proteins for each EV subpopulation were subsequently identified (Figures 4e and S3, Table S12).
The analysis identified PBA_c5 (markers: LGR5 and SIGLEC5) and PBA_c7 (markers: CD151 and CD81) as AF4‐specific EV subpopulations. Exo‐CMDS‐specific EV subpopulation included PBA_c6 (markers: CD5, NT5E, TNFRSF9 and ITGB2). SEC‐specific EV subpopulations included PBA_c1(marker: APOE), PBA_c9 (marker: THBD) and PBA_c11 (markers: CDH12, SELL and CLMP). Several EV subpopulations were specific to the plasma‐control group, including PBA_c2 (markers: APOE, CD36 and ITGA1), PBA_c8 (markers: DSG1 and DSG2), PBA_c12 (markers: L1CAM and APOE), PBA_c13 (markers: ITGA4B7 and TACSTD2) and PBA_c14 (markers: APOE and CD36). Of note, the expression levels of CD5 and CD151 were highest in PBA_c6 and PBA_c7, respectively (Figure 4f). Correlation analysis of the bulk EV protein expression matrix across the 15 EV subpopulations showed a strong correlation between AF4‐specific clusters PBA_c5 and PBA_c7 (Spearman correlation coefficient = 0.58, p < 0.05, Figure 4g). While, SEC and plasma‐control clusters showed greater similarity to each other, indicating an overlap in the types of EVs isolated by SEC and those present in unprocessed plasma (Figure 4g).
Proteome Profiling of EVs by LC‐MS/MS
To provide an in‐depth analysis of EV‐associated proteins isolated by different technologies, we performed LC‐MS/MS to quantify the entire EV proteome. A total of 1588 proteins were quantified across all samples (Table S4). For subsequent analysis, we focused on proteins that were detected in at least 60% of the samples within each technology group. As a result, we obtained 1514 proteins in the AF4 group, 583 in Exo‐CMDS and 500 in SEC (Figure 5a). Across these groups, 370 proteins were found to be common. In general, the EVs by AF4 exhibited the highest protein expression levels compared to Exo‐CMDS and SEC (Figure 5b). No significant differences were observed in the distributions of protein expression levels across the different technology groups or between the freeze‐thaw and non‐freeze‐thaw samples within each technology group (Figure 5c).
The LC‐MS/MS analysis revealed that CD9 and CD81 were robustly detected across all three technology groups and ranked among the proteins with the highest expression levels (Figure 5b,d). Their expression levels were highest in the AF4 group, consistent with our PBA analysis results (Figure 3f). In contrast, CD63 was not detected in the Exo‐CMDS group, and its expression levels varied significantly in the SEC group, likely due to low protein concentrations impacting LC‐MS/MS detection accuracy (Figure 5d). Additionally, we also evaluated the expression levels of EV‐related markers reported by MISEV2023 (Welsh et al.) (Figure S4, Table S13). Specifically, AF4 samples detected charged multivesicular body protein (CHMP) family proteins and VPS4A/B, associated with the ESCRT complex (Christ et al.). AF4 and SEC samples contained FLOT family proteins, related to EV biogenesis and development (Phuyal et al.). BSG, an ectosome marker (Mathieu et al.), was detected in AF4 and SEC samples. We also detected the protein expression of the non‐vesicular extracellular particles (NVEPs) markers such as lipoproteins (APOA1/2 and APOB) and exomere/supermere proteins (TGFBI, HSP13, LDHA and LDHB) (Figure 5e,f, Table S14). The expression levels of APOA1/2 and APOB were higher in Exo‐CMDS and SEC groups than AF4 group, suggesting that the particles isolated by Exo‐CMDS and SEC might include more lipid particles than AF4 (FDR < 0.05). In addition, the expression levels of TGFBI, HSP13, LDHA and LDHB were higher in AF4 group than the other two groups, indicating that the particles isolated by AF4 might include more exomeres/supermeres than the other two technologies (FDR < 0.05).
PCA plot revealed a clear separation of EV samples isolated by different technologies (Figure 5g). The hierarchical clustering of sample‐wise correlations showed that the EV samples isolated by the same technology were clustered together and there were no significant differences in protein expression patterns of EV samples between freeze‐thaw and non‐freeze‐thaw samples (Figure 5h). The Exo‐CMDS group showed the highest consistency in protein expression, with an average Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.99, followed by AF4 and SEC with the same coefficient of 0.97 (p < 0.05, Figure 5i). These findings were consistent with PBA result (Figure 3c), which also showed higher reproducibility of EV populations by Exo‐CMDS. In terms of technology‐specific proteins, 909 were exclusively detected in AF4 samples, 34 in Exo‐CMDS and four in SEC (Figure 5a, Table S15), which we denoted as AF4‐detected‐only, CMDS‐detected‐only and SEC‐detected‐only proteins. To further evaluate differentially expressed proteins, we focused on the 640 proteins detected in at least two technology groups (Figure 5a). In total, we identified 240 AF4‐elevated proteins, 108 Exo‐CMDS‐elevated proteins and 71 SEC‐elevated proteins (p < 0.05, Table S16). A ternary plot characterized the expression patterns of these technology‐specific proteins (Figure 5j).
Clustering and Functional Annotation of EV Proteome Profiling
To better understand the co‐expression patterns and functional significance of these technology‐specific EV proteins, we further conducted a hierarchical clustering analysis on the 1588 proteins quantified by LC‐MS/MS, which resulted in the identification of 17 distinct protein clusters (MS_c1‐17, Figure 6a, Table S17). AF4‐detected‐only proteins were significantly enriched in clusters MS_c1, MS_c5, MS_c11, MS_c12 and MS_c16 (hypergeometric analysis, p < 0.05, Figure 6b). Exo‐CMDS‐detected‐only proteins were significantly enriched in MS_c4. SEC‐detected‐only proteins were significantly enriched in MS_c14. Proteins expressed across all three technology groups were most significantly enriched in cluster MS_c6.
A detailed investigation into technology‐specific proteins revealed key markers associated with particular EV subpopulations. For instance, CD81 was enriched in AF4‐elevated cluster MS_c3 and was also the marker protein for the AF4‐specific EV subpopulation PBA_c7 (Figure 6c, Table S17). APOE was enriched in SEC‐elevated cluster MS_c13, corresponding to the SEC‐specific EV subpopulation PBA_c1. Lipoprotein markers, such as APOA, ALB and APOB, were enriched in Exo‐CMDS‐elevated and SEC‐elevated cluster MS_c13 (Table S17). Notably, a total of 48 surface proteins, including CD81 and APOE, were quantified by both PBA and LC–MS/MS (Figure S5). Among these, 19 proteins showed a significant positive correlation (Pearson correlation, p < 0.05), underscoring the similarity between the two methods.
Functional enrichment analysis of the identified clusters provided additional insights into the distinct characteristics of EV subpopulations isolated by different technologies (p < 0.05, Figure 6d). Proteins in AF4‐enriched clusters (MS_c1, MS_c5, MS_c11, MS_c12, MS_c16) were significantly associated with nuclear‐derived components, such as nucleoplasm, nuclear lumen and ribonucleoprotein complexes. In addition, AF4‐elevated cluster MS_c3 showed significant enrichment in cell‐substrate junction components, such as focal adhesions and anchoring junctions. AF4 also captured EVs enriched in proteasome‐related proteins (MS_c2). Exo‐CMDS exhibited significant enrichment for proteins associated with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus, notably in cluster MS_c4, relating to intracellular trafficking and protein synthesis. Furthermore, Exo‐CMDS‐elevated clusters MS_c6 and MS_c15 were enriched for extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including collagen‐containing ECM and platelet granule markers. In contrast, proteins in SEC‐elevated cluster MS_c13 were significantly enriched in protein‐lipid complex, immunoglobulin complex and blood microparticles, consistent with the frequent co‐isolation of non‐vesicular lipid particles by SEC.
Tissue specificity analysis revealed that proteins from the commonly expressed cluster MS_c6, along with Exo‐CMDS‐elevated and SEC‐elevated proteins, particularly those in MS_c13, were significantly enriched in liver, which was consistent with previous reports indicating that liver is a primary source of circulating EVs (Arraud et al.; Nieuwland and Siljander; Wiklander et al.) (Figure 6e, Table S18). Single‐cell specificity analysis further demonstrated that Exo‐CMDS‐ and SEC‐elevated proteins, particularly those from MS_c13, and MS_c6, were enriched in hepatocytes, aligning with the tissue specificity results. Additionally, SEC‐elevated proteins, along with half of the proteins from cluster MS_6 and cluster MS_13, were enriched in plasma cells (Figure 6f, Table S18).

INTRODUCTION:
Introduction
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are small, membrane‐bound particles that play crucial roles in intercellular communication by transferring a variety of biomolecules such as DNA, RNA, proteins and lipids (Kalluri and LeBleu; Raposo and Stahl; Théry et al.; Willms et al.; Xu et al.). These particles are critical in various physiological and pathological processes, including immune responses, tissue repair and disease progression (Hoshino et al.; Tkach and Théry; Willms et al.; Zhang et al.). EVs are highly heterogeneous, varying in size, biogenesis, molecular composition, surface markers and functional roles. Typically, they are classified into small EVs (<200 nm) and large EVs (200–1000 nm) (Welsh et al.). Among small EVs, exosomes (30–150 nm) are of particular interest due to their endosomal origin and their significant potential in diagnostics and therapeutics (Hoshino et al.; Ji et al.; Lin et al.; Lin et al.; Negahdaripour et al.; Xu et al.; Yu et al.). Unless otherwise specified, the EVs discussed in this study refer to small EVs.
Blood‐derived EVs, particularly plasma EVs, hold great potential in precision medicine, as they are easily accessible and contain a variety of biomolecules from diverse tissues, organs and cell types (Kalluri and LeBleu; Raposo and Stahl; Théry et al.; Xu et al.). Plasma EVs have been shown to carry molecular biomarkers that can aid in disease diagnosis, stratification of treatment response and prognosis in various pathological conditions, such as metastatic breast cancer (Tian et al.), and early‐stage pancreatic, gastric, glioblastoma and colorectal cancers (Lou et al.; Osti et al.; Tian et al.; Zhang et al.). Furthermore, specific plasma EV subpopulations have demonstrated associations with the early development of Alzheimer's disease and colorectal cancer (Guo et al.; Cai et al.).
Despite these advancements, a major challenge in plasma EV research is the lack of standardized protocols for EV isolation (Möller and Lobb). Commonly used EV isolation methods include size‐based, density‐based, precipitation and immunoaffinity‐based techniques (Shirejini and Inci). Each method yields EVs with variable purity, efficiency and subpopulation diversity, complicating cross‐study comparisons and data reproducibility. Traditional methods such as ultracentrifugation (UC) and density gradient or cushion often suffer from high impurity levels, low efficiency and the need for large sample volumes(Burton et al.; Monguió‐Tortajada et al.; Shirejini and Inci). Size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) is another widely used approach that isolates EVs based on size by passing the sample through a column packed with porous beads (Guo et al.). Compared to UC and density gradient/cushion, SEC is relatively quick, gentle on EVs, and does not require large sample volumes (Guo et al.; Shirejini and Inci). It also provides high reproducibility and is scalable for different sample sizes (Guo et al.). However, SEC can result in the co‐isolation of similarly sized non‐vesicular extracellular nanoparticles (NVEPs), such as protein aggregates and lipoprotein particles, which may require additional isolation steps (Benayas et al.; Karimi et al.; Karttunen et al.; Zhang et al.).
Recent advancements in EV isolation have led to the development of more refined techniques. Asymmetric flow FFF (AF4) is a high‐resolution isolation technique that fractionates extracellular nanoparticles based on their hydrodynamic sizes via perpendicular channel and cross flows (Zhang et al.). This technique not only ensures gentle handling and high reproducibility but also supports real‐time monitoring of nanoparticles (Monguió‐Tortajada et al.; Serrano‐Pertierra et al.; Zhang and Lyden). In addition, microfluidic technologies have emerged as powerful tools for EV isolation, allowing precise manipulation of small sample volumes. Of note, a recently developed automated centrifugal microfluidic disc system combined with functionalized membranes (Exo‐CMDS) utilizes centrifugal force‐driven microfluidic flow to isolate EVs through a three‐step process: microfiltration removes blood cells and large particles, electrostatic interactions capture EVs on functionalized membranes, and centrifugal elution selectively separates and harvests purified EV fractions (Zhao et al.). This process achieves high‐purity isolation primarily through centrifugal separation, with filtration and charge‐based adsorption serving as additional mechanisms to enhance the purity and speed of the isolation (Strohmeier et al.; Zhao et al.). Both approaches have been reported to achieve high separation efficiency and have demonstrated applicability across multiple disease contexts (Monguió‐Tortajada et al.; Serrano‐Pertierra et al.; Zhang and Lyden).
Although these novel methods represent progress, systematic comparisons of their effects on EV yield, purity and subpopulation characteristics are still limited. Most studies focus on bulk EV populations, overlooking the diversity and functional differences of EV subpopulations (Guo et al.; Hu et al.; Liang et al.; Oeyen et al.; Zhang and Lyden). Recent advances, such as the proximity barcoding assay (PBA), enable high‐resolution profiling of surface proteins on individual EVs (Wu et al.). This technique employs antibody‐conjugated DNA probes, where protein binding triggers DNA amplification through rolling circle amplification, allowing the detection of multiple EV surface proteins simultaneously. By converting protein presence into quantifiable DNA sequences, PBA provides detailed insights into EV subpopulation diversity (Cai et al.; Guo et al.; Kong et al.; Min et al.; Sun et al.; Zhai et al.). Notably, this approach allows quantification of up to 262 distinct membrane proteins on individual EVs while requiring only 10 µL of plasma. Besides, another underexplored aspect of EV research is the impact of sample handling, particularly freeze‐thaw cycles, on EV integrity and functionality. Given the frequent use of freeze‐thaw processes for storage, understanding EV stability under these conditions is crucial for developing protocols that ensure consistency in downstream analyses.
In this study, we systematically compared three complementary EV isolation techniques, including AF4, Exo‐CMDS and SEC, in terms of their efficiency in isolating plasma‐derived EVs and capturing distinct EV subpopulations. These methods were chosen for their clinical applicability, scalability to large sample cohorts, low plasma volume requirements and high isolation efficiency. We utilized a combination of advanced methods, including PBA for single‐EV surface profiling, liquid chromatogram‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) for comprehensive proteomic analysis, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) for particle size and concentration, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for EV morphology assessment. Our results revealed distinct EV subpopulations associated with each isolation method, providing a deeper understanding of their characteristics. In addition, we evaluated the effects of freeze‐thaw handling on the isolated EVs, showing that a single freeze‐thaw cycle did not significantly impact EV size or composition across the isolation techniques, which is crucial for practical plasma sample handling and storage. By elucidating the effects of isolation methods and freeze‐thaw handling on EVs, our study contributes valuable insights for the development of robust EV protocols in both research and clinical applications.
