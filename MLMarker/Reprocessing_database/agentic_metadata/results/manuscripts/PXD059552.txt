ABSTRACT:
The cellular surfaceome is crucial for cellular homeostasis and communication, with surface proteins acting as key receptors, transporters, and enzymes. However, comprehensive analysis of the surfaceome is challenging due to the hydrophobic nature and low abundance of membrane proteins, resulting underrepresented in mass spectrometry-based proteomic analyses. This study introduces a novel method for profiling the cell surfaceome using furan-biotin (FB) conjugates. We synthesized six FB compounds (FB1-FB6) and showed that FB1 reacts with lysine and cysteine in close proximity. FB1 exhibited the highest staining intensity and specificity for surface proteins upon oxidation. Protein pull-down assays confirmed the efficiency and reproducibility of FB1 in enriching surface proteins, outperforming traditional NHS-biotin methods by reducing nonspecific labeling of intracellular proteins. This method provides a robust tool for unbiased, high-specificity proteomic studies of cell surface dynamics, particularly under oxidative conditions, and opens new avenues for exploring cell surface interactions and protein modifications.

PROJECT DESCRIPTION:
Cell Surface Biotinylation via Furan Crosslinking to Profile the Surfaceome

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
Cell labeling and pull-down assays. After cell labeling with the furan-biotin probes, cell lysis and avidin pull down assays were performed as previously described (REF book). Briefly, cells growing in 100-mm plates until reaching subconfluence were washed three times with ice-cold PBS. Then, each plate was incubated for 15 min on ice with 5 ml of PBS containing 500 µM sulfo-NHS-biotin (EZ-Link™ Sulfo-NHS-biotin, Thermo Fischer Scientific) or 500 µM furan-biotin on ice. The control samples were incubated with DMSO (0.4%, 5 ml/dish) as the stock solutions of FB and the sulfo-NHS-biotin are dissolved in. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with 10 ml of ice-cold PBS. Cells were lysed by adding 1 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM 2-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazin-1-yl]ethanesulfonic acid pH 8.0, 0.5% DOC, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) 150 mM NaCl, 1X protease inhibitor (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and scrapped. The lysates were collected and sonicated 9 times for 2 s at 30 kHz and left in an end-over-end rotator for 30 min at 4 °C. Lysates were centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 × g at 4 °C and supernatants collected. Protein concentration was determined by standard Bradford assay. 2 mg of protein adjusted to a 2 ml volume with lysis buffer was mixed with 50 µl of avidin resin (previously equilibrated with lysis buffer) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated in an end-to-end rotor for 2.5 hours at 4°C. Then, beads were precipitated for 3 min at 800 × g at 4 °C and washed three times with lysis buffer by incubating each wash for 15 min at 4 °C. After, beads were washed with 1 ml of urea buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 M urea) for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by incubation with 1 ml of reduction and alkylation buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 M urea, 20 mM iodoacetamide, 10 mM TCEP.HCl pH 8.0) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Beads were washed once with lysis buffer followed by a wash with and 50 mM TEAB. The proteins were digested on beads overnight at 37°C in 200 µl of 50 mM TEAB and 1.5 µg of trypsin (Promega, Fitchburg, Wisconsin, USA). Following digestion, beads were pelleted and the supernatant containing the digested peptides was transferred to new LoBind® Eppendorfs and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added to a final concentration of 0.1% TFA to stop trypsin digestion. The peptides were then cleaned up with C18 ZipTips (Ziptip Bond Elut OMIX; Agilent, Santa Clara California, USA) and dried in a vacuum centrifuge.

DATA PROCESSING:
Liquid-Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry Analysis. The tryptic peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system in-line connected to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientic). Trapping of the mixture was performed in loading solvent A (0.1% TFA in water:acetonitrile (ACN) (98:2, v:v)) on a 20 mm trapping column (made in-house, 100 µm internal diameter (I.D.), 5 µm beads, C18 Reprosil-HD, Dr. Maisch, Germany). Peptide separation was on a 200 cm µPAC™ column (C18-endcapped functionality, 300 µm wide channels, 5 µm porous-shell pillar, interpillar distance of 2.5 µm and depth of 20 µm; Pharmafluidics, Belgium). A linear gradient of solvent B (0.1% FA in water:ACN (2:8, v:v)) reaching 99% solvent B at 150 minutes was used to elute the peptides. The flow rate started at 750 nl/min and was decreased after 15 min to a constant flow rate of 300 nl/min. Data-dependent mode was enabled on the mass spectrometer to automatically switch between MS and MS/MS acquisition for the most abundant ion peaks in a given MS spectrum. Full-scan MS spectra (300-1500 m/z) were acquired in 3 s acquisition cycles at a resolution of 120,000 in the Orbitrap analyzer after accumulation to a target AGC value of 200,000 with a maximum injection time of 30 ms. The precursor ions were filtered for charge states (2-7 required), dynamic range (60 s; +/- 10 ppm window) and intensity (minimal intensity of 3E4). The precursor ions were selected in the multipole with an isolation window of 1.2 Da and accumulated to an AGC target of 5E3 or a maximum injection time of 40 ms and activated using HCD fragmentation (34% NCE). The fragments were analyzed in the Ion Trap Analyzer at normal scan rate.
