ABSTRACT:
The integration of omics technologies into molecular biological research has transformed it into a field where a system can be investigated as a whole by combining information from multiple biochemical layers. This extension of traditional molecular biology, where various data sources are considered independently, offers novel insights into complex molecular interactions. Here, we present a multi-omics analysis of murine brown adipocytes undergoing thermogenic lipolysis, which is part of the non-shivering response to cold. Through combining information from multiple types of biomolecules, we describe what is important for each biochemical layer, and the system as a whole, at different time points. Furthermore, a classification of data integration approaches for multi-omics data analysis is presented.

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
Immortalized murine brown preadipocytes were were grown in 6-well plates with DMEM media (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 in a humidified incubator. For differentiation, cells were grown until confluence (day 0) and subsequently treated with differentiation media. On day 0, fresh growth media supplemented with an induction cocktail of 20 nM insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), 1 nM triiodothyronine (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 µM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.125 mM indomethacin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 mM isobutylmethylxanthine (Sigma-Aldrich). After 2 days of cultivation (day 2), the media was changed to growth media supplemented with 20 nM insulin and 1 nM triiodothyronine. The media was changed every 2 days (day 4 and day 6). All experiments were performed on day 7. Cells were stimulated with 1 µM L-(−)-norepinephrine (+)-bitartrate salt monohydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) and samples were taken at the specified sampling time points.  Samples were taken for metabolomics, transcriptomics and proteomics prior to induction of lipolysis (0 h) and 4 h and 24 h after stimulation with norepinephrine. Additional samples were taken for metabolomics analysis at 30 min, 1 h, 2 h and 8 h after stimulation. For proteomics, samples were harvested by removing the media, washing each well with 1 mL PBS, lifting the cells off the plates using cell lifters, resuspending them in 500 µL PBS, centrifuging them at 300 g and removing the supernatant so that only the cell pellet remains, and frozen at -80 °C until further processing.  Frozen cell pellets were lysed with chaotropic denaturing guanidinium-HCl lysis buffer (6 M guanidine-HCl, 50 mM Tris pH 8.5). 5 mM TCEP and 10 mM chloroacetamide (CAA) were added to lysates for methionine alkylation and reduction of disulfide bonds, respectively. To shear DNA, lysates were sonicated on ice for 20 seconds at 35% amplitude. After 1 hour incubation at room temperature, proteins were digested into peptides by incubating with Lys-C endopeptidase (1:200 w/w; Wako Chemicals) for 3 hours, followed by overnight incubation with modified sequencing-grade Trypsin (1:200 w/w; Sigma Aldrich) after diluting to 2M guanidine-HCl with 25 mM Tris pH 8.5. Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added to a final concentration of 0.5% v/v to inactivate proteases, and samples were centrifuged at high speed to remove precipitates. Peptides were purified using four-layer C18 stage tips (Empore High performance extraction disks, 3 M, No. 2215) as follows: StageTips were activated with 100 µl Methanol and equilibrated twice with 100 µl of 80% Acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% formic acid (FA), after which samples were loaded. StageTips were then washed with 100 µl 0.1% FA, eluted with 40% ACN in 0.1% FA, and vacuum-dried in a SpeedVac at 60 °C. Peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% Formic acid for mass spectrometry analysis.  Mass spectrometry experiments for proteomics were performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (MS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using high-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) fragmentation. Samples were analyzed on 15 – 20-cm long analytical columns with an internal diameter of 75 µm, packed in-house using ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ 1.9 µm beads (Dr. Maisch, Ammerbuch, Germany) connected to a nanoscale EASY-nLC 1200 liquid chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The analytical column was heated to 40 °C using a column oven and peptides were eluted from the column with a gradient of buffer A (0.1% formic acid) and buffer B (80% ACN in 0.1% formic acid). The primary gradient ranged from 5% to 38% buffer B over the course of 60 minutes, followed by an increase to 48% buffer B over 5 minutes, a further increase to 90% buffer B over 2 minutes, constant 90% buffer B for 2 minutes, decrease to 5% buffer B over 3 minutes, and 5% buffer B for 5 minutes. Electrospray ionization (ESI) was achieved using a NanoSpray Flex NG ion source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Spray voltage was set to 2 kV, capillary temperature to 275 °C, and RF level to 40%. Full scans were performed at a resolution of 120,000, with a scan range of 300–1750 m/z, maximum injection time set to auto, and a normalized AGC target of “200”. Isolation was performed at a width of 1.3 m/z, normalized AGC target of “200”, and precursor fragmentation using HCD at 25% collision energy. Top 18 precursors with charge state 2–6 were isolated for MS/MS analysis, and a dynamic exclusion of 60 s was used. MS/MS spectra were measured in the Orbitrap, with maximum precursor injection time set to auto and scan resolution of 15,000.

Cells were grown in 6-well plates with DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) media (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.
For differentiation, cells were grown until confluence (day 0) and subsequently treated with differentiation media. On day 0, fresh growth media supplemented with an induction cocktail of 20 nM insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), 1 nM triiodothyronine (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 μM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.125 mM indomethacin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 mM isobutylmethylxanthine (Sigma-Aldrich). After 2 days of cultivation (day 2), the media was changed to growth media supplemented with 20 nM insulin and 1 nM triiodothyronine. The media was changed every 2 days (day 4 and day 6). All experiments were performed on day 7. Cells were stimulated with 1 μM L-(−)-norepinephrine (+)-bitartrate salt monohydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) and samples were taken at the specified sampling time points. The cells tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.
Sampling
Samples were taken for metabolomics, transcriptomics and proteomics prior to induction of lipolysis (0 h) and 4 h and 24 h after stimulation with norepinephrine. Additional samples were taken for metabolomics analysis at 30 min, 1 h, 2 h and 8 h after stimulation.
For metabolomics, the cells needed to be quenched and the metabolites extracted. A working solution of ice-cold 90% methanol with internal standards (0.441 mg/L valine D8, 22.06 mg/L glutamic acid 13C5 15N, 22.06 mg/L palmitic acid D31, 2.21 mg/L 3-hydroxybutyric acid 13C4, 0.22 mg/L succinic acid D4, 1.25 mg/L decanoic acid D19, 0.378788 mg/L phenylalanine 13C9 15N, 1.25 mg/L vitamin B5 13C5 15N, 1.25 mg/L oleic acid D17) was used. Labeled standards were acquired from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. (Tewksbury, MA, USA).
Cells were taken from the incubator and kept on ice for the following steps. The media was transferred to reaction tubes and each well was washed with 1mL PBS (phosphate buffered saline, prepared in-house). After removing the PBS, 500 μL of ice-cold 90 % methanol working solution was added. Cells were scraped off the well bottoms using cell scrapers. The cells were collected in the methanol solution and transferred to reaction tubes. For metabolite extraction, the cell suspensions were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, thawed at 4 °C and vortex at least three times. After incubation at 4 °C for one hour for protein precipitation, the suspensions were centrifuged at 16 000 g at 4 °C for 15 min and the cell debris pellet was separated from the methanolic extract supernatants, which were stored at -80 °C.
For proteomics and transcriptomics, samples were harvested by removing the media, washing each well with 1 mL PBS, lifting the cells off the plates using cell lifters, resuspending them in 500 μL PBS, centrifuging them at 300 g and removing the supernatant so that only the cell pellet remains. The pellets were split in two and frozen at -80 °C until further processing.
Method details
Metabolomics
Metabolomics data was acquired using four separate methods; GC-MS (gas chromatography mass spectrometry), reverse phase LC-MS, HILIC (Hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography) LC-MS (liquid chromatography mass spectrometry) and derivatized sugar phosphates measured using LC-MS.
Dried extracts of 50 μL of sample for GC-MS-TOF (time-of-flight) analysis were silylated by derivatized with 12.5 μL methoxamine (MOX, 2% in pyridine, Thermo Fisher Scientific), incubated at 45 °C for 60 min and combined with 12.5 μL N,O-Bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide with 1% trimethylchlorosilane (BSTFA with 1% TMCS, Trimethylsilyl chloride, Thermo Fisher Scientific) by a Gerstel MSP multisampler (Mülheim an der Ruhr, Germany), and again incubated at 45 C for 60 min. Prior to injection, 50 μL of 10 mg/L−1 4,4′-dibromooctafluorobiphenyl in hexane were added an injection standard. The measurement was performed with a Leco Pegasus BT GC/TOFMS instrument (Leco Corp., Saint Joseph, Michigan) using a Restek Rxi 5 ms column (pn:13423–6850) with 1.2 mL·min−1 of helium flow and an inlet temperature of 270°C. The chamber was held at 40 °C prior to injection, then kept for 1 min before being accelerated at 20°C/min to 340°C, where it remained stable for 3 min. Mass fragmentation occurred at 70 eV with an electron ionization source. Mass spectra were acquired by the detector in scan mode from 50 to 750 Da at 10 Hz. Raw output files were converted into centroid mode and exported as netCDF files before being extracted using a targeted in-house library with software from the Swedish Metabolome Center. Retention times were aligned and metabolites were isolated based on an in house metabolite library of retention times and fragmentation patterns.
For the reverse phase- and HILIC analysis, 200 μL of samples were dried and reconstituted in 50 μμL before use. First, the reverse phase analysis was performed using an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column, 100 Å, 1.8  μm, 2.1 mm by 100 mm (Waters, Milford, MA) in an ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system Agilent 1290 Infinity II (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) connected to a Bruker timsTOF Pro instrument equipped with a trapped-ion mobility spectrometer (TIMS) coupled to a hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TOF-MS) (Bruker, Bremen, Germany), in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. Water (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 0.1% formic acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for solvent A and a 3:1 (vol/vol) mix of acetonitrile and 2-propanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 0.1% formic acid. The column and autosampler temperatures were maintained at 40 °C and 4 °C, respectively. The following gradient was applied: 0 min 3 % B, from 0 min to 9 min to 100 % B, 9 min to 14 min 100 %, 14 min to 14.5 min to 3 % B, 14.5 min to 17 min 3 % B, with a flow of 0.4 mL/min. The VIP-ion source used 10 liters/min of drying gas at a temperature of 220°C, set at a 3500-V capillary voltage and 300 kPa nebulizer pressure. A m/z range of 50 to 1000 over 17 min was detected at at 2 Hz. Data was processed using MetaboScape version 5.0 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) using an in-house metabolite library. Only features that appeared in at least half of all replicates of each time point were considered for further analysis. Sodium formate was used as an internal calibrant.
Sugar phosphates were derivatized following a method described in Rende et al. In brief, 50 μL samples were dried together with 50 ng of Fructose 6-phosphate 13C as an added standard before being oxomylated followed by propionylation. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 20 μL MOX (methoxamine, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. 6 μL 1-Methylimidazole (Sigma Aldrich) were added as a catalyst, together with 12 μL of the reactant propionic anhydride before incubating for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were dried for 30 min and reconstituted in water with 0.1 % formic acid. The samples were analyzed with an Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column, 100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm by 100 mm (Waters, Milford, MA) on a Premier Acquity UPLC (Waters) connected to a Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters) equipped with an electrospray ionization source. Samples were randomized prior to data acquisition. The following separation was applied. Solvent A: water with 0.1 % formic acid. Solvent B: acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid. Data was acquired from 0 min to 14 min. The following gradient was applied: 0 min 0.1 % B, from 0 min to 0.54 min 0.1 % B, from 0.54 min to 3.5 min increase to 30 % B, from 3.5 min to 6 min increase to 35 % B, from 6 min to 8 min 35 % B, from 8 min to 10 min increase to 70 % B, from 10 min to 12 min increase to 99 % B, from 12 min to 14 min 99 % B, from 14 min to 14.5 min 0.1 %, from 14.5 min to 17.5 min 0.1 % B with a flow of 0.5 mL/min.
The samples were measured in negative mode with a capillary voltage of 0.5 kV and a cone voltage 40 V. Desolvation temperature was 400 °C with a gas flow of 800 L/h and cone gas flow of 150 L/h. The MRM (multiple reaction monitoring) settings were the same as previously described in Rende et al. The data was processed with the Waters TargetLynx software using standard settings.
For HILIC, the same setup as for reverse phase with the following changes was used. The VIP-ion (vacuum insulated probe) source used 10 liters/min of drying gas at a temperature of 220°C, set at a 3600-V capillary voltage and 220 kPa nebulizer pressure. A m/z range of 50 to 1000 over 20 min was detected at at 3 Hz. The same samples as for reverse phase were measured with an Atlantis Premier BEH (Ethylene Bridged Hybrid) Z-HILIC 1.7 μm VanGuard Fit 2.1 by 100 mm column and the following gradient. Solvent A: water with 10 mM NH4HCO3. Solvent B: acetonitrile with 10 mM NH4HCO3. Gradient: 0 min 95 % B, 0 min to 2 min 95 % B, 2 min to 12 min to 55 % B, 12 min to 14 min 55 % B, 14 min to 14.5 min to 95 % B, 14.5 min to 20 min 95 % B, at a flow of 0.2 mL/min. The HILIC data was processed using MzMine 3. The raw data was converted to mzML files using msconvert of the ProteoWizard software suite with an intensity cut-off of 1000 to remove background noise. Data was imported and mass detected using the centroid algorithm. Targeted features were detected based on an in house metabolite library. The intensity tolerance was set to 15 %, the m/z tolerance scan-to-scan to 0.008 m/z, and the retention time tolerance to 0.1 minutes. The data was aligned using the standard procedure of using twice the tolerances and exported for further analysis.
For all analyses, metabolites with missing values in more than 50 % of the replicates per time point were discarded. Given the small sample size and the consistent signal observed across pooled QC samples, normalization was considered unnecessary and therefore not applied.

Transcriptomics
Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA sequencing was performed by the Single-Cell Omics platform at the Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Basic Metabolic Research. Libraries were prepared using Universal Plus Total RNAseq with NuQuant kit (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) as recommended by the manufacturer. In brief, 250 ng total RNA was fragmented followed by cDNA synthesis. Adaptors were ligated and after stand selection, the cDNA was depleted from rRNA using AnyDeplete probes (Tecan). Following the final bead clean-up, libraries were quantified with NuQuant, quality checked using a TapeStation instrument (Agilent Technologies) and subjected to 52-bp paired-end sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The data was aligned with the nf-core RNAseq workflow version 3.8.1. Read counts were further processed using edgeR and limma and visualized with Glimma, following Law et al.
Quantification and statistical analysis
Differential abundance analysis
For metabolomics, absolute fold changes of 2 and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values of two sample t-tests below 0.01 were set as requirements to label an element as differentially abundant. For proteomics data analysis, the dataset was reduced to proteins appearing differentially abundant after performing two sample t-tests, of 0 h vs. 4 h, 0 h vs. 24 h and 4 h vs 24 h, and a Benjamini-Hochberg-FDR (false discovery rate) of 0.05 was set as a significance threshold. with an adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05 and a fold change of an absolute value of 2. Additional information about the function and involvement of biological elements was found on NCBI(National Center for Biotechnology Information), HMDB (Human Metabolome Database), UniProt. Enrichment analyses were performed using GSEApy.
mixOmics
The PLS-DA model was constructed using the mixOmics R package. The model was set up, fine tuned and tested according to an example analysis using the DIABLO method described on the mixOmics website. Multiple models were constructed and evaluated and ultimately a model based on z-scores of log2 transformed data was selected. The model was evaluated using a sparse PLS-DA with the DIABLO method. The model consists of 2 components with 10 elements per omics layer, with the correlations in the design matrix set to 0.1. Gene set enrichment analysis was applied on the omics layers from the two components in the DIABLO model using R package multiGSEA.
MOFA
The MOFA model was constructed and analyzed following examples and advice on the MOFA website using its R implementation. The data was log2 transformed to approximate a Gaussian distribution and a model constructed with z-scores was selected. Multiple models were compared. The top results were the same as for a model built on non-scaled data but the z-score model could explain a higher amount of variance. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed on the omics layers from the two factors in the MOFA model using R package multiGSEA.
Transomics
Elements were pre-filtered to be highly significant and differential; the cutoff was set to a log2 fold change higher than 1 or lower than -1 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value smaller than 0.05. Transomics networks were created by linking information from multiple databases together. Transcriptomic information was expanded with Ensembl data using the PyEnsembl Python package. Further gene information, such as NCBI gene IDs, was retrieved using the MyGene API by BioThings, from which also the MyChem API (Application Programming Interface) was used to connect ChEBI, InChI and PubChem metabolite identifiers, which were furthermore linked to KEGG compound IDs using BioServices. Protein information was retrieved from UniProt, and supplemented by protein-protein interaction data from STRING, transcription factor information from ChIP-Atlas and information about activators and inhibitors from BRENDA. KEGG provided connecting information and information about reactions and pathways. The models were constructed and analyzed with custom python scripts and their outputs visualized with Arena3Dweb.

RESULTS:
Results and discussion
A time series experiment was performed to capture the transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics responses to the induction of thermogenic lipolysis. Metabolomic changes happen very rapidly, so multiple time points were recorded to trace the progression of the metabolic adaptation. Transcriptomic and proteomic adjustments take longer to manifest, so a control 0 h sample, a 4 h and a 24 h sample were recorded for each type of sample (Figure S1). Six replicates were taken per omics layer and time point.
Post-analysis data integration
Multiple time-point samples were collected for metabolomics analysis to evaluate the temporal progression of the adipocytes’ response to the induction of thermogenic lipolysis. A principal component analysis (PCA) of all the samples illustrates a metabolic shift occurring 4 h after norepinephrine stimulation. Furthermore, it illustrates the similarity of all the time points up until and including 2 h to the uninduced 0 h samples. The 8 h samples, taken after the metabolic shift at 4 h, lie between the early time points and the 24 h samples, potentially separated from the latter due to the cells’ responses to sustained stimulation and prolonged cultivation time. A heatmap of all the time points revealed one cluster of metabolites that differ between 8 h and 24 h, decreasing over time (see Figure S2). These metabolites are media components; most are amino acids, and there is also glucose and fatty acids which can be found in FBS. After the cells transition from the metabolic state at 4 h to the sustained norepinephrine stimulation metabolic state at 8 h and 24 h, they likely don’t metabolically rearrange anymore and only consume media. After 24 h, much of these media components are consumed and thus the bioavailable concentration decreases, which can explain why these media components are more abundant intracellularly at 8 h than at 24 h.
This means that, based on the metabolomics data, we observed 3 distinct metabolic states: uninduced (0 h), active lipolysis (4 h) and sustained induction (24 h). PCA of these three time points for each omics type shows similar distributions, indicating that these time points capture the metabolic rearrangement during a time course of thermogenic lipolysis (Figure 2A). Similarly oriented separations could be observed for each omics dataset individually, with minor differences in component 1 (see Figures S4 and S9). The primary variation in metabolome and transcriptome data describes differences between all three time points, while proteomics data highlight differences between active lipolysis and the other conditions. First, the results of each individual omics analysis will be discussed, and then their results will be combined and compared in the “summary post-analysis data integration” section.
Metabolome
The contribution of metabolites to the first two components of PCA is shown in Figure 2B, with component 1 mainly characterizing the metabolic shift 4 h after the induction of lipolysis, and component 2 describing sustained induction. Differential abundances are visualized in Figure S3.
The metabolites contributing the most to separating the 4 h time point from the others are significantly lower abundant at 4 h. These are l-leucine, an amino acid that is metabolized into acetoacetic acid which has been described to be a defining metabolite of thermogenic lipolytic activity in brown adipocytes, 3-hydroxyglutaric acid, which is formed from glutaric acid, l-phenylalanine, an amino acid, o-butanoylcarnitine, which along with other carnitines, is involved in fatty acid transport into mitochondria and uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) which is used to form glycans and glycerolipids. Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolites also decrease, including l-malic acid and fumaric acid, the aforementioned acetoacetic acid, and galactose-1-phosphate, which plays a role in balancing glucose-1-phosphate and UDP-glucose. One important fatty acid contributing to the separation is oleic acid, which peaks at 4 h and decreases at 24 h.
The separation of sustained lipolysis from the others is largely defined by the decreased nucleosides inosine, and increased cytidine and the fatty acids palmitic acid and arachidonic acid, which peak at 4 h and decrease at 24 h. Many TCA and central carbon metabolism intermediates, such as lactic acid, increase over time, as does the glycolysis intermediate fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. As for the carnitines, l-carnitine, propionylcarnitine and o-acetylcarnitinum are decreased at 24 h, while only oleoylcarnitine is increased compared to 0 h. Metabolites such as 5-methoxy-5-oxopentanoic acid, which is closely connected to glutaric acid, also contributes to the separation.
Proteome
The overall proteome composition of the analyzed brown adipocyte cell line was visualized for the 0 h time point in Figure S5. Visually, hardly any differences could be detected when comparing it to the other time points, indicating that its most abundant proteins do not drastically change over time (see Figures S6 and S7).
To get a functional understanding of the proteomics rearrangements happening between the different time points, differential abundance analysis was performed, visualized in Figure S8. The data was then furthermore separated into six clusters by k-means clustering (Figure 3A) to generate interaction networks in Cytoscape via the stringApp. Enrichment analyses were then applied on each cluster to prioritize enrichments of functions that are shared by known interaction partners.
In cluster 1 (Figure 3B), proteins were higher in abundance at 4 h than at the other time points. There is a strong enrichment of proteins related to mRNA (messenger RNA) metabolism, cellular stress response, and, possibly related to the latter, proteins involved in the immune system. Notable proteins include Ndufa1, which is involved in the respiratory chain, and Kif3b, partaking in intracellular transport, Pdk4, a fatty acid metabolism regulating kinase, and Plin2, a structural component of lipid droplets that are potentially released when droplets decrease in size.
Cluster 2 consists of proteins that are significantly in higher abundance at 24 h than at the other time points. This cluster shows increased levels of proteins associated with TCA metabolism, fatty acid β-oxidation, and amino acid metabolism which includes the ketone body metabolic process. Two distinct clusters participating in the TCA metabolism and in fatty acid β-oxidation are identified in the interaction networks, with their relative abundances at each time point highlighted in Figure 3C. Finally, proteins associated with sustained lipolysis are the collagens Col1a1-2 and Bgn; enzymes Pnpla2-3, which catalyzes the initial step of triglyceride hydrolysis in adipocytes and non-adipocyte lipid droplets, Tmem70, a transmembrane protein that binds the mitochondrial proton-transporting ATP synthase complexes I, Smap, involved in clathrin-dependent endocytosis, the coagulation factor III F3, Impdh1, which converts inosine-5-phosphate to guanine nucleotide precursors, Cct6b, which assists in protein folding, and Cxcl12, a T-lymphocytes and monocytes attracting chemokine.
Clusters 3 and 4 consist of proteins whose abundance is highest at 0 h. Cluster 3 proteins, which are least abundant at 4 h, are enriched in: mitochondrial translation elongation, protein targeting to mitochondria, respiratory electron transport, mRNA splicing and organellar large ribosomal subunit (Figure 3D). Many of these proteins are associated with mitochondrial activity.
Cluster 4 proteins, which exhibit the lowest levels at 24 h, are primarily involved in lipid and fatty acid metabolism (Figure 3E). Proteins such as Impdh1, Itm2b, and Plin2 show a significant decrease over time. They are involved in inosine 5-phosphate conversion to guanine nucleotide precursors, which are structural components of lipid droplets. Proteins that are at their highest levels prior to the induction of lipolysis, i.e., decrease after stimulation with norepinephrine include Scd2, a desaturase introducing double bonds into fatty acids; Ube2g2, a ubiquitin acceptor; Alas1, which is involved in succinate metabolism; Pnkd, a hydrolase activating NF-κB; Pex11a, a peroxisomal membrane protein; Mcrip2, which is involved in RNA metabolism; and Tpx2, which partakes in the assembly of the mitotic spindle.
Clusters 5 and 6 did not reveal distinct patterns.
Transcriptome
Identified significantly differentially expressed genes are summarized in an UpSet plot (Figure 4A), where overlaps between the sets of genes are highlighted. To better understand the functional implications of these changes, general enrichment analyses using the camera R package and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) for KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and Reactome terms (see Figure S10), were performed on the sets of differentially expressed genes from the pairwise comparison to identify enriched functions, pathways and other ontological terms.
Seven genes are significantly different in each of the comparisons (Figure 4B), most of them steadily decreasing over the whole induction period. These genes are Ero1a, Sema3g, Egln3, Ffar4, Tlcd3b, and they are related to fat metabolism and oxidoreductase activity, which might also play a role in fatty acid metabolism. Specifically, Tlcd3b and Sema3g are targets of PPAR-gamma, the master regulator of lipid metabolism; Ffar4 encodes receptors for free fatty acids; Egln3 is involved in the apoptotic process, which might be because differentiated cells at the end of their life cycle are being stressed.
Two genes, G0s2 and Ptch2, which are involved in apoptotic signaling and may be involved in tumor suppression, respectively, significantly decrease in 4 h compared to 0 h before significantly increasing again at 24 h. This might be the result of an acute stress response at 4 h. Cholesterol biosynthesis was underrepresented at 4 h. Cytochrome p450 oxidoreductase (POR) targets are overexpressed and overrepresented at 0 h compared to 4 h, as in triglyceride biosynthesis, glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, and wound response. The GSEA indicates a reduction of steroid- and terpenoid biosynthesis, glycerolipid- and galactose metabolism, and fat digestion and absorption, as well as an increase of rRNA processing after norepinephrine stimulation. Ucp1, a mitochondrial protein that uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, facilitating heat generation in brown adipose tissue, was differentially higher expressed on the transcript level after sustained NE stimulation.
Between 4 h and 24 h, genes that are part of the respiratory electron transport are downregulated at 24 h, potentially as a response to the sustained induction of lipolysis, and so are genes related to the TCA cycle and Alzheimer’s disease, which might be representative of a general stress response. Genes related to rRNA (ribosomal RNA) processing, translation, and cholesterol biosynthesis also increase at 24 h. The ontologies for adipogenesis, on the other hand, appear downregulated.
Summary post-analysis data integration
The post analysis data integration shows various responses to thermogenic lipolysis induction, with each omics layer highlighting both common pathways and unique aspects of cellular regulation.
The metabolomics data indicate that nucleosides and -breakdown products play an important role during lipolysis, with the involvement of metabolites closely connected to glutaric acid (e.g., 3-hydroxyglutaric acid and 5-methoxy-5-oxopentanoic acid). Fatty acids and diverse carnitines define the separation of the different time points, highlighting their role in facilitating mitochondrial fatty acid transport. The decrease of TCA cycle intermediates during the metabolic shift related to active lipolysis further suggests a shift in substrate utilization and energy metabolism. The proteomics data indicate that the defining factor differentiating the 4 h time point from the others is the breakdown of many proteins. Proteins regulating fatty acid metabolism and structural components of lipid droplets are at their highest levels at 4 h after norepinephrine stimulation, revealing the early mobilization of cellular machinery to manage energy demands and lipid droplet dynamics. Furthermore, TCA and fatty acid β-oxidation related proteins are highly abundant after sustained stimulation, indicating a transition toward sustained energy production and metabolic adaptation. For transcriptomics, a wide variety of differentially expressed genes involved in metabolic processes could be found, many of which could be contextualized and related to previous studies. Early time points are characterized by the overrepresentation of genes related to cholesterol biosynthesis, glycolysis, and fatty acid metabolism, with many of these genes steadily decreasing before active lipolysis. During sustained induction, there is an upregulation of genes associated with respiratory electron transport, the TCA cycle, and rRNA processing, suggesting an overall shift toward enhanced mitochondrial activity and protein synthesis. Interestingly, many differential genes have unknown functions, indicating novel regulatory mechanisms that might be specific to thermogenic lipolysis. Collectively, these independent analyses of the different omics datasets and subsequent comparison of results paint a picture of increased release of fatty acids and transport into mitochondria via carnitines, as well as the dynamic reorganization of energy production pathways during lipolysis.
Integrated data analysis
Two different approaches were used for integrated data analysis: one supervised, PLS based method, and one unsupervised, factor analysis. The supervised analysis optimizes the covariance structure between omics datasets while also considering the relationship between these datasets and a response variable, which will specifically identify elements that separate the investigated conditions. The unsupervised analysis performs pattern mining across omics datasets, capturing the shared and dataset specific variability, and is well suited for exploratory data analysis. These separate analyses split the time points differently, meaning that they focused on different aspects of the data. This indicates that they complement each other, and that using more than one method diversifies the obtained results. Their results will be combined and compared in the “summary integrated analysis” section.
PLS-based integration
The PLS-based integration was performed on the three omics datasets using the DIABLO (Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent cOmponents) model in R package mixOmics. Testing an initial model revealed that even a minimal set of components and elements per omics layer would be sufficient to differentiate the measured time points. Ultimately, a two component model with 10 elements per omics layer and component was constructed.
The resulting model can separate the input time point samples perfectly, with no ambiguity in classification indicated by the distance of separation of the clusters (see Figure S11). Dimension 1 separates the 4 h (metabolic shift caused by lipolysis) from the 0 h (uninduced) and 24 h (sustained induction) conditions. Dimension 2 follows the temporal progression, separating the samples by differences between uninduced and sustained stimulation with norepinephrine. The weighted contributions of the selected elements to each component can be seen in the contributions plots in Figure 5.
Overall, component 1 identified elements related to cellular stress and response to the induction of thermogenic lipolysis, and component 2 identified elements directly involved in thermogenesis.
Component 1: Cellular stress and response to the induction of thermogenic lipolysis
The transcriptome (Figure 5A1) shows that at 4 h, several genes, such as Hivep1, a transcriptional repressor of NF-κB, are elevated, indicating an effort to balance NF-κB activity and mitigate stress. In contrast, genes such as Cyp51 and those involved in lipid droplet formation (e.g., Sqle and Fdft1) exhibit lower expression at 4 h, possibly reflecting the rapid metabolism of lipid droplet contents. Additional genes, including Ankrd28 and Mtss1, further describe the acute response, with Ankrd28 increasing, potentially modulating nitric oxide levels under low oxygen conditions, and Mtss1 decreasing, suggesting a suppression of differentiation pathways during stress.
Metabolites contributing to component 1 (Figure 5A2) are either amino acids or involved in fatty acid metabolism. For example, arachidonic acid, oleic acid, and l-palmitoylcarnitine peak at 4 h, emphasizing enhanced fatty acid mobilization and mitochondrial transport 4 h after norepinephrine stimulation. Conversely, several amino acids, including l-tyrosine, l-phenylalanine, and l-proline, are at their lowest levels at 4 h, suggesting rapid utilization or not sufficient synthesis during this acute phase. It has been reported that high amounts of lactate are released upon the stimulation of lipolysis, which could serve as a possible explanation for why l-lactic acid is at its lowest level at 4 h intracellularly. Also low at 4 h are fumaric acid, a TCA cycle intermediate, potentially due to the uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation; N-acetyl-l-serine, which is involved in the terminal post translational modification of proteins, could be related to the increased production of new proteins after norepinephrine stimulation.
All top 10 proteins in Component 1 (Figure 5A3) are positively associated with the 4 h time point. Proteins such as Acat2, involved in cholesterol ester formation and lipid droplet assembly, and Prkar1a, which is involved in cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) signaling and thus part of the activation of thermogenic lipolysis, are elevated. This contrast of elements involved in lipid droplet formation being decreased at 4 h on a transcript level, while being increased on the protein level, might reflect the temporal difference in adaptation time of the respective biological layers. Tkt, of the pentose phosphate pathway, and its loss has been described to promote lipolysis, increases at 4 h, suggesting its role in balancing lipolysis. Increased chaperones (Hsp90ab1, Cct8) and protein synthesis mediators (Etf1, Eif2a, Arcn1) further indicate a surge in protein translation and folding in response to lipolysis stimulation.
Component 2: Elements involved in thermogenesis
Component 2 captures elements that steadily decrease or increase over time, splitting up all conditions. All of the identified top 10 genes (Figure 5B1) are of higher abundance at 0 h than at the other time points. Here, genes such as Atp2a3, which regulates intracellular Ca2+ levels by encoding Serca3, which transports Ca2+ ions from the cytosol into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Serca3 is a paralog of Serca2, which is the main protein involved in thermogenic Ca2+ futile cycling. Here it appears to be expressed at its highest level before the induction of thermogenic lipolysis, which is contrary to a previous report stating that it is expressed upon induction. Others include genes encoding ER (endoplasmic reticulum) membrane associated proteins are Gramd1b, which binds cholesterol, and Edem1, a mannosidase involved in regulating protein transport and a degradation enhancing enzyme in the ER. The transmembrane amino acids carrier encoded by Slc3a2, which has been suggested to be potentially relevant for protein synthesis and -degradation during futile cycling, also decreases over time. Same for Zfand5, which is involved in protein degradation, in this case via ubiquitination. Previous literature postulates that glycolytic enzymes are involved in the regulation of the function of brown adipose tissue, and that the deletion of Pgam1, a gene for a glycolytic enzyme which here appears to decrease after the induction of lipolysis, leads to the whitening of adipocytes. Active brown adipocytes need a lot of oxygen supply to function. It is thus possible that induction of thermogenic lipolysis causes hypoxia, and that the hypoxic response is exhausted after 24 h, which could explain why Egln3, a hydroxylase that activates HIF-1α, (hypoxia-induced factor 1α) the master regulator of hypoxia response, is associated with the uninduced time point.
Most of the top metabolites contributing to Component 2 (Figure 5B2), except for L-carnitine, L-glutamine, and tetradecanoic acid, have relatively low correlation to transcripts and proteins, exhibiting different temporal patterns with similar levels at 4 h and 24 h. L-carnitine is known to promote thermogenesis and is also involved in fatty acid transport by derivatization, and thus likely used up or in high turnover after norepinephrine stimulation. The fatty acid tetradecanoic acid, or myristic acid, is likely broken down in fatty acid β-oxidation during thermogenic lipolysis.
Proteins contributing to component 2 (Figure 5B3) are involved in two major mechanisms. Many are involved in broad lipid-, fatty acid- and cholesterol metabolism, such as Hsd17b4, Scp2, Acaa1a, and Lss, all of which are all at their highest levels before the induction of lipolysis and decrease over time. The others, Sf3b1, Sf3a3, Tubb4b, Ddx5, Sf3b3, Smu1, are involved in mRNA splicing or other RNA metabolism mechanisms. All are at their highest levels at 0 h except for Tubb4b, which reaches its highest abundance at 24 h. Also, Ddx5 has been previously described to be important in adipogenesis, which might explain why its abundance decreases after the induction of lipolysis.
A heatmap of the elements contributing the most to separating the conditions in the PLS analysis can be found in Figure 5C.
Factor analysis based integration
For factor analysis, an MOFA (multi-omics factor analysis) model was constructed to incorporate the three omics data, with the sample time points as metadata. The number of factors and which factors were described in detail were selected based on the procedure described in the documentation of MOFA, specifically the tutorial based on the original article. Given the limited number of replicates, we selected a conservative model with 4 factors. More factors were tested, but they did not explain meaningful amounts of variance (below 2%). Additional diagnostic- and result figures can be found in the supplements, Figures S12–S14.
The resulting factors do not appear to correlate much, with only factor 1 and 2 both showing some correlation with factor 3, indicating that factor 3 describes a pattern partially shared with these factors (see Figure S14). In general, the resulting model explains more than 40% of the variance in each omics layer. Most of the variance is explained by factors 1 and 2 (Figure 6A). Interestingly, factor 3 seemingly identified an outlier transcriptomics sample and mostly separated it from the rest, which indicates that this was the biggest source of variance after the information that was used to set up factors 1 and 2 was removed (see Figure S12). In combination with the small spread of the sample groups in factors 1 and 2, this furthermore indicates that the outliers did not contribute to these factors. Factor 4 does not separate the samples themselves (see Figure S12). Additionally, most of the variance is described by the first two factors. Based on this, only factors 1 and 2 were declared to be “active” factors and used for further analysis. Specifically, factor 1 and factor 2 separate the sample groups by induced vs. not induced and the metabolic shift related to active vs. sustained lipolysis, respectively. Factor 1 identified elements involved in thermogenic lipolysis in brown adipocytes while factor 2 seemingly describes the difference between acute and sustained stimulation with norepinephrine (Figure 6B).
The weight distributions for each omics dataset and factor (see Figures 6C1 and 6C2) exhibited linear patterns at the extremes, with the weights of the most contributing elements having an absolute value close to 1, indicating that most features have non-zero weights and contribute to the factors, and many elements contribute strongly. The top 10 elements of each omics layer and each factor were selected for closer inspection, most of which have associated weights above 0.9.
Factor 1: Elements involved in thermogenic lipolysis
Genes contributing to factor 1 include those involved in signaling and metabolic regulation. For example, Alkal2, which regulates the Erk1 and Erk2 cascade, is associated with lipolytic time points (4 h and 24 h). Other genes decrease in expression after stimulation, such as Srebf1, which is involved in sterol biosynthesis, Egr3, a transcription factor, Itga7, an integrin, Acss2, an acyl-CoA (acyl-coenzyme A) synthetase which is involved in lipogenesis, and Amer1, which is potentially part of adipose tissue development. Interestingly, Lpin1, known for its role in fatty acid oxidation and thermogenic lipolysis, decreases in expression after induction, whereas Net1, which is linked to DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) stress response, is upregulated after stimulation.
The metabolites contributing the most to factor 1 peak at 0 h. These include uric acid, a purine breakdown product linked to obesity, and other urine related products such as 3-hydroxyglutaric acid and 5-methoxy-5-oxopentanoic acid, both formed from glutaric acid. Another two of the contributing metabolites are I-leucine, a ketogenic amino acid and its main metabolic end product acetoacetic acid, a ketone body, which is generally assumed to be mostly formed in liver cells, but it has been shown that brown adipocytes can derive it from glucose. Two carnitine derivatives were also found, o-butanoylcarnitine and oleoylcarnitine, which carry fatty acids into mitochondria, showing opposing trends here, the former increasing over time while the latter decreases. It has been suggested that both glycogenesis and glycogenolysis are activated upon chronic cold exposure over the course of multiple days. Here, higher abundance of glucose-1-phosphate, the end product of glycogen breakdown, was observed prior to norepinephrine stimulation. Two TCA metabolites, citric acid and l-malic acid, are positively associated with the 0 h time point indicating either higher flux or lower formation during lipolysis. Finally, pantothenic acid (vitamin B5), which is essential in the synthesis of CoA and as such for both fatty acid synthesis and -degradation, is also among the top contributors to factor 1.
Proteins related to factor 1 are involved in DNA specific processes. Fen1 is active in DNA replication and repair, here associated with the time points after the induction of lipolysis, possibly in response to increased cell stress. Hells, involved in DNA methylation and Smarca5, involved in DNA replication, and in the recruitment of DNA methyltransferases for rDNA silencing also contribute. Two proteins related to lipids; Plin2, a structural component of lipid droplets, and is needed for formation and maintenance, showed increased abundance in response to lipid droplet size changes during lipolysis. On the other hand, Gnpat, which synthesizes plasmalogens, a type of glycerophospholipid, decreases after induction. Other proteins decrease in expression include Glud1, the glutamate dehydrogenase which provides TCA intermediates, Pdk4, a kinase that regulates fatty acid metabolism by inhibiting acetyl-CoA formation in favor of the TCA cycle, which also leads to increased fat metabolism, Alyref, an mRNA nuclear export protein. Tmpo, which is part of the nucleus structure) and Ndufb1, a part of the respiratory electron chain, showing the extensive molecular reprogramming captured by factor 1.
Factor 2: Difference between acute and sustained stimulation with norepinephrine
Factor 2 describes the separation of active- (4 h) from sustained lipolysis (24 h), with the uninduced time point (0 h) in the middle. The expressions of these elements show the complete opposite direction at 4 h and 24 h, indicating some functional shift happening between active and sustained lipolysis. Here, two genes involved in ubiquitination, Peli2 and Mylip, can be found to be associated with the 4 h time point, along with Pnrc1, a proline-rich nuclear receptor coactivator, Btg1, which negatively regulates cell proliferation, Ccng2, a cyclin, and BC005537, a predicted gene, indicating its possible involvement in or response to lipolytic activity. Higher expression after sustained induction is seen in Adamts5, the gene for a member of a multi-domain matrix-associated metalloendopeptidases family, and Tpst, which encodes a protein-tyrosine sulfotransferase. There are also two genes that align with the cells response to the induction of lipolysis; Pde4b, the gene for a phosphodiesterase involved in cellular response to epinephrine, is expressed higher at active lipolysis and Adrb3, the gene for the beta-3 adrenergic receptor, which is the receptor whose stimulation activates thermogenic lipolysis, is expressed higher at 24 h, indicating that the receptor is re-expressed after prolonged induction.
For the metabolome, inosine and adenine are among the highest weighted in factor 2. Both are closely connected to cAMP, the main intracellular signaling molecule in response to norepinephrine stimulation. The main metabolite class contributing the most to factor 2 is carnitines; o-stearoylcarnitine, o-dodecanoylcarnitine, isovalerylcarnitine, and l-palmitoylcarnitine are positively associated with the 4 h time point, and propionylcarnitine is associated with sustained induction. These temporal patterns might indicate that the utilization of specific fatty acids is different when comparing active-to sustained lipolysis. Additionally, glucose-6-phosphate is at its lowest at 4 h, either because of enhanced glycolysis flux or a lower glucose uptake rate. Two metabolites that are involved in the browning of adipose tissue, 2-oxovaleric acid and 3-methyl-2-oxopentanoate, are at their highest levels after 24 h, which could be an indication that they are formed in response to the activation of thermogenic lipolysis. Finally, l-threonic acid, an isomer of vitamin C which is probably derived from glycated proteins or the degradation of ascorbic acid, is positively associated with the 4 h after norepinephrine stimulation time point.
Most of the proteins contributing the most to factor 2 are involved in coenzyme A and/or are part of glucose metabolism. The proteins whose functions involve coenzyme A and peak at 4 h are Gcdh, a part of the lysine and tryptophan degradation pathway, and Acly, which cleaves citrate into oxaloacetate and acetyl-CoA. On the other hand, Acads, which is involved in fatty acid beta oxidation, and Suclg2, a part of TCA cycle, are highly abundant at 24 h. Two more enzymes participating in the TCA cycle could be found: Me1, malic enzyme, which is associated with active lipolysis despite its role in lipogenesis, and Idh3b, which is associated with the sustained induction of lipolysis. Furthermore Vps13c, a mitochondrial lipid transfer mediator, and Fasn, a fatty acid synthase, are highest at 4 h while Pck2, which is involved in gluconeogenesis and potentially more abundant due to the depletion of bioavailable glucose levels, and Vwa8, the von Willebrand factor A domain-containing protein 8 usually involved in wound response, are more abundant at 24 h.
Pathway analysis
To further compare different types of integrated analysis to post-analysis integration, GSEA was performed on 2 components from DIABLO and 2 factors in MOFA, see Table S1. The results reveal distinct yet interconnected biological processes across different omics layers. Unlike post-analysis integration, which often identifies isolated molecular events, integrated methods capture a coordinated biological response, reflecting systemic adaptations rather than independent changes within individual omics layers.
Extensive molecular remodeling and cellular adaptation to stress during lipolysis
In DIABLO component 1, the shared characteristics of the 4 h time point across the three omics is the extensive molecular remodeling and cellular adaptation to stress, with each omics highlighting different aspects of this response. The transcriptome shows increased ribosomal biogenesis and RNA processing, reflecting an upregulation in protein synthesis machinery to meet the elevated metabolic and stress-related demands. The proteome suggests an elevated translational capacity and intracellular trafficking, facilitating protein synthesis and vesicular transport necessary for cellular adaptation under stress. Meanwhile, the metabolome emphasizes immune system activation, which aligns with proteomics findings showing oxidative stress responses and increased immune activity. This suggests that at 4 h after norepinephrine stimulation, brown adipocytes reallocate resources from basal metabolic processes toward stress management and protein remodeling, ensuring cellular function under thermogenic conditions.
Shift in mitochondrial energy generation over time
Component 2 in DIABLO illustrates a shift in mitochondrial energy generation over time. The transcriptome shows the downregulation of the TCA cycle and respiratory electron transport, suggesting a transition away from ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation. However, proteomics data indicate an upregulation of mitochondrial function, possibly due to the compensatory changes in glycolysis and lipid metabolism reflected by the metabolome, suggesting a reorganization of metabolic pathways to balance energy demands. These findings highlight a transition from reliance on proton-gradient-driven ATP synthesis to mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, ensuring a sustained energy supply during prolonged lipolysis.
Enhanced translational capacity and stress responses in lipolysis
MOFA Factor 1 captures the shared characteristics between active and sustained lipolysis, characterized by enhanced translational capacity and stress responses. This closely aligns with DIABLO Component 1, with minor differences in pathway ranking, suggesting that the cellular adaptation mechanisms established at 4 h after norepinephrine stimulation persist throughout the induction period. The sustained high levels of translation-related pathways indicate that cells continue to divert resources from energy production toward protein synthesis, remodeling, and stress adaptation, ensuring the maintenance of thermogenic function over time.
Shift in cellular priorities and metabolic adaptations from active to sustained lipolysis
MOFA Factor 2 highlights the major differences between active and sustained lipolysis, emphasizing shifts in cellular priorities and metabolic adaptations. At 4 h, transcriptomic data reveal a sharp increase in mitochondrial activity and energy production, while proteomics data suggest that these processes become more prominent at sustained lipolysis, indicating a temporal delay in protein-level realization. During active lipolysis, proteomics data highlight a transition from cell growth to cellular maintenance, marked by increased protein breakdown, remodeling, and activation of stress signaling pathways. Metabolomic analysis further distinguishes the 4 h time point by capturing rapid energy mobilization, heightened neurotransmitter turnover, and stress-related metabolic pathways, reflecting an acute response to norepinephrine stimulation.
In contrast, sustained lipolysis is characterized by enhanced protein synthesis and glycolysis-gluconeogenesis cycling, which support prolonged thermogenic activity through the increased engagement of futile cycles. This metabolic transition is also evident in the transcriptomic profile, where brown adipocytes shift from an acute stress response to a more controlled, energy-efficient metabolic state. At this stage, the upregulation of translation initiation and nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) ensures precise protein turnover, while cholesterol biosynthesis plays a crucial role in maintaining membrane integrity and lipid homeostasis. Overall, these findings suggest that the metabolic state at 4 h, which is related to active lipolysis, is driven by rapid energy mobilization, stress adaptation, and metabolic reprogramming to sustain acute thermogenic activation, whereas sustained lipolysis prioritizes energy efficiency, protein quality control, and cellular maintenance to support prolonged thermogenic function.
Summary integrated analysis
Both the PLS-based method and the factor analysis approaches identified metabolites, proteins, and transcripts that confidently differentiate the time points, but they achieve this through distinct strategies. The PLS-based method explicitly prioritizes correlated changes among different omics layers, capturing elements likely involved in the same pathways or processes. For example, the PLS-based analysis focus on the distinguishment of active lipolysis (4 h) from uninduced (0 h) and sustained (24 h) conditions, highlighting markers such as elevated Hivep1 (a regulator of NF-κB activity), reduced expression of lipid droplet formation genes such as Sqle and Fdft1, and alterations in central carbon metabolism that collectively point to acute cell stress and hypoxia as prominent effects of thermogenic lipolysis. In contrast, factor analysis decomposes the overall variability, emphasizing broad systems-level rearrangements. Rather than directly aligning with experimental conditions, it identifies intrinsic patterns, such as shifts in energy metabolism and stress response, that differentiate the time points. The factor analysis uncovered key processes, such as CoA-related pathways during lipolysis, that underpin the global metabolic reorganization. Despite their different strategies, both methods identified shared patterns: elements involved in differentiation and cell maintenance are at their highest levels before stimulation with norepinephrine, while the 4 h time point is marked by low lipid droplet formation and high cell stress. Moreover, both 4 h and 24 h show signs of hypoxia, and specific carnitines and elements involved in central carbon metabolism and protein synthesis exhibit distinct temporal patterns, indicating the metabolic difference between active and sustained stimulation. One concern lies in the sample size; the number of samples used here was small for both the PLS-based method and the factor analysis, which is why results should be treated as indications, suggestions for follow up studies and further investigation rather than as proven findings.
Transomics
For the transomics analysis, mechanistic network models of functionally connecting biological elements were constructed by cross linking information retrieved from multiple databases. The network was reduced to only include measured elements and connecting information, such as annotated reactions, and further limited to elements that are connected to the smallest dataset, the metabolomics data. A representation of the reduced transomics network can be seen in Figure S15.
Interestingly, all of the higher abundant metabolites after the induction of lipolysis also appear to have annotated regulatory functions, contrary to the metabolites that decrease in abundance in these comparisons (see Table 1).
The most important use of transomics networks is to trace changes in abundance throughout the network and elucidate signaling events. Here, the full network was reduced to only include elements connected to reactions included in the KEGG pathway for the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes and in the fatty acid metabolism pathway. The visualized networks can be found in the supplements (Figures S16 and S17).
The only protein with differentially higher abundance in any comparisons in this pathway is Rock2, the Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase 2, and two metabolites, the fatty acid arachidonic acid and the glycolysis metabolite fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. Both metabolites serve as activators for the same reaction, which is annotated by the same EC (enzyme commission) number as Rock2, 2.7.11.1. It is thus likely that protein Rock2 is further activated by arachidonic acid and fructose 1,6-bisphosphate to execute the ATP:protamine O-phosphotranspherase reaction, where protamine is phosphorylated with ATP to o-phosphoprotamine. Protamine has been shown to prevent obesity in rats by inhibiting lipase activity and thus triglyceride hydrolysis. It is unclear what effect phosphorylation has on protamine’s inhibitory function but this finding indicates a potential involvement of protamine in the response to thermogenic lipolysis in brown adipocytes.
Another pathway that yielded notable results was the fatty acid metabolism pathway. The correspondingly reduced transomics network mostly shows differential elements in its related genes, all of which are kinases; three of them are involved in upper glycolysis and are decreased in expression, Pgk1, involved in ATP production during glycolysis, and Pfkl and Pfkp, which both phosphorylate fructose 1,6-bisphosphate during glycolysis. One of the differential genes, Pklr, which has been described to regulate lipid metabolism, appears highly expressed after the induction of lipolysis. It is involved in lower glycolysis and catalyzes the reaction of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate. This points to decreased activity of upper-, and increased activity of lower glycolysis in response to the induction of thermogenic lipolysis.
Summary transomics
The comparison of information from multiple databases revealed that most of the higher abundant metabolites after the induction of lipolysis appear to also exhibit a regulatory function. A closer look at the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes pathway revealed a potential involvement of protamine, a lipase inhibitor, and arachidonic acid, the starting metabolite of the eicosanoid pathway, in the response of brown adipocytes to thermogenic lipolysis. Comparing the expression levels of genes involved in fatty acid metabolism indicates the down regulation of upper glycolysis and upper regulation of lower glycolysis might be upregulated during thermogenic lipolysis. These results also highlight why transomics is particularly useful for multi-omics studies that include metabolomics data, since the integration of network information was able to generate links that might be missed by most generalist methods, such as pathway enrichment analysis. Metabolomics data tends to be more sparse than other omics data, and changes in metabolite levels are more complex than, e.g., changes in transcript levels. This issue has been expertly discussed in a recent publication. A network-based analysis such as transomics functions complementary to other multi-omics integration methods and can highlight specific elements that might otherwise be overlooked.
Transomics network interpretation can further be improved by arranging the network according to annotated pathways, as is presented in Kokaji et al. and can be done using the tools presented in Nishida et al. or Sugimoto et al.

INTRODUCTION:
Introduction
Biological systems operate on multiple layers, which are investigated in so called “omics” fields, for example transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics. These approaches aim to record the respective layer as entirely as possible. Multiple different omics data sets can be combined in multi-omics studies, in order to observe the system holistically. Any combination of two or more omics datasets is commonly referred to as a multi-omics analysis. This causes two semantic issues, (1) what type of information is included, i.e., which omics measurements were performed and (2) how the data are analyzed. Efforts have been made to classify these analyses based on how the biological information is combined.
By taking the existing literature into account, the following categorization of approaches to integrate multi-omics datasets to study metabolism is suggested: post-analysis data integration, integrated data analysis (further split into linear/non-linear- and supervised/unsupervised methods), transomics, knowledge graphs, and system modeling (Figure 1).
All these methods have different strengths and weaknesses for specialized applications that are discussed later in discussion.
In post-analysis data integration, the datasets are analyzed separately, and findings are correlated. This is the most frequently published type of multi-omics analyses and works well for the comparison of two or more functional states (e.g., induced vs. control).
Typical analyses include differential abundance tests, where usually two sets are compared at a time. Enrichment analysis, which compares a specific set of genes or proteins of interest to a reference set built on ontologies, is also commonly used as a subsequent analysis. This analysis statistically assesses whether biological terms (such as gene functions, pathways, or ontologies) are significantly overrepresented in the gene/protein set of interest compared to the reference, revealing potential functional associations.
Integrated data analysis methods aim to identify the biggest sources of variation by evaluating the provided omics datasets simultaneously. A tradeoff between model complexity and practical applicability should be considered; while large models built on extensive data might perform best, their applicability can be limited. For example, routine checks in clinical settings rarely support comprehensive multi-omics profiling of every patient due to constraints in time and resources. In such situations, a simpler model based on a smaller, well-selected set of variables would be preferred and are more clinically relevant.
Integrated approaches can further be separated into linear- and nonlinear methods. Linear methods are different approaches to construct linear combinations of variables. In contrast, nonlinear methods, typically based on machine learning and neural networks, are capable of modeling more complex patterns. Although these methods can capture both linear and nonlinear relationships without prior assumptions, their strength lies in modeling complicated nonlinear patterns that linear approaches cannot adequately capture.
Most multivariate statistics-based multi-omics analysis methods are based on projection of latent structures or partial least squares (PLS). PLS, similar to principal component analysis (PCA), separates data based on variance but constructs composite variables correlated to a response vector. In other words, PLS is better at generating explanatory variables that highlight the differences between defined groups, while PCA highlights independent trends. This underlines that PLS is best used to create explanatory separations and classification models, while PCA and similar methods are more unbiased.
For factor analysis, a selected number of unmeasured factors, which are weighted combinations of elements of the input data, are identified. It is thus more similar to a PCA than a PLS analysis, making it a suitable tool for exploratory data analysis.
It should be noted that factor analysis models are most useful for large sample sizes, and that one should consider both the number of variables involved in the analysis and the number of factors to be fit when selecting the sample size.
The application of large(r) machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods highly depends on the amount of available input data.
In general, neural networks are useful to identify underlying patterns in large datasets, and a network can be optimized to describe anything that can be expressed in terms of a loss function. Their biggest limitation lies in the amount of available annotated training data. Also, training a model requires much more computing power compared to the other approaches discussed here.
Embeddings are low-dimensional projections of high-dimensional information. It is well suited for dimensionality reduction and finding similar elements in a large set of samples.
In single-cell omics analysis, embeddings such as UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) cluster cells, identifying different cell types that show similar distribution patterns across the different biological elements that were measured.
A similar approach is the use of Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), which aim to encode- and subsequently decode information via a latent low-dimensional space in between. This latent space can then be probed to reveal elements showing similar- and different behaviors, and intermediate states. Successful applications of this in biological research include the following: Drug-omics association could be described for a cohort of patients newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, graph networks have been applied for biomarker identification and multiple studies have been conducted to classify and cluster cancer types and disease progression, many of which have been benchmarked in Leng et al.
In transomics, biological elements of multiple omics measurements are functionally connected in multilayer networks. Each layer represents one omics type or biochemical information (e.g., reactions). These networks are generated based on database information on known metabolic reactions, molecular interactions, and pathways. For example, a protein can be connected to a gene that encodes it, to a gene that it acts as a transcription factor for, or to a metabolite that it interacts with. Transomics combines prior knowledge about mechanistic connections with experimental data, making it a powerful method to investigate regulation and signal flow.
Transomics networks can be used for multiple applications, of which a non-exhaustive list and descriptions can be found in Yugi et al. Past successful applications have included the elucidation of obesity specific glucose-response regulation, and combining flux-with phosphoproteomics data and reaction regulation information in a kinetic-transomics network to investigate a system’s reaction in response to a stimulus.
Knowledge graphs are structured representations of entities and their relationships, organized as interconnected nodes and edges. They can be used to integrate multi-omics data to form context-relevant networks that capture relations and interactions between functionally connected biological entities, facilitating data exploration and hypothesis generation. Their applications include the contextualization of omics measurements in the biochemical network, systems level annotation of- and interaction with biochemical elements, predictions of disease outcomes and contextualization of treatments and more.
Metabolic models are reconstructions of the biochemical reaction network, which are used for flux predictions to estimate how quickly parts of the metabolic network operate.
The simplest form of metabolic models is constraint-based models, where the network is encoded in a stoichiometric matrix. Computationally, this can be expressed as a linear programming issue, where flux distributions satisfying given constraints are predicted to optimize an objective function, most commonly maximizing growth. Kinetic models are more complex, requiring more detailed- and mechanistic input, but clearly outperform constraint-based models when predicting perturbations and temporal patterns. More information on the different types of metabolic models can be found in Volkova et al.
Metabolic models usually do not incorporate multi-omics data but are constructed based on it. Depending on the complexity of the model, different experimental data types can be added to inform its predictions.
Although multi-omics studies can reveal systems-level information, it is difficult to make meaningful claims about causation without dedicated cause-and-effect experiments.
Lipolysis is the fundamental metabolic process by which triglycerides are sequentially hydrolyzed into glycerol and free fatty acids through the action of three key enzymes: adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), and monoacylglycerol lipase (MGL). This process is essential for mobilizing stored energy during periods of energy demand, such as fasting, exercise, or cold exposure, and serves as a critical communication mechanism between adipose tissue and other organs. In adipocytes, lipolysis is tightly regulated by hormonal signals, particularly being stimulated by catecholamines such as norepinephrine and epinephrine, while being inhibited by insulin. The complexity of lipolytic regulation extends beyond simple enzymatic cascades to involve intricate protein-protein interactions, post-translational modifications, and spatial organization within lipid droplets.
To demonstrate the applications of various multi-omics approaches, we set up an exploratory hypothesis generation study focusing on thermogenic lipolysis in brown adipocytes. Unlike white adipocytes, which store energy in the form of lipids, brown adipocytes participate in the non-shivering thermogenic cold response, generating heat by expending energy.
Thermogenic lipolysis represents a specialized form of lipolysis that occurs specifically in brown adipose tissue and is characterized by both the breakdown of triglycerides and the coupling of this process to heat generation through uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) activation.
Thermogenic lipolysis is an induced effect, triggered by the recognition of norepinephrine by the beta-adrenergic receptor on the surface of brown adipocytes. However, it is important to recognize that beta-adrenergic receptor activation initiates a complex signaling cascade that affects multiple cellular functions beyond lipolysis, including changes in gene expression, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the activation of various metabolic and signaling pathways. For instance, β-adrenergic stimulation also enhances glucose uptake, activates cyclic AMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA), and promotes the expression of thermogenic genes, which together contribute to the overall metabolic remodeling of brown adipocytes. Therefore, laboratory experiments using β-adrenergic agonists reflect a broader physiological response.
In vivo, norepinephrine is secreted by the brain upon cold exposure, activating brown fat as part of the non-shivering cold response. Intracellularly, a phosphorylation cascade leads to the activation of lipases which catalyze the initial digestion of triglycerides and the release of fatty acids, which activate the mitochondrial uncoupling protein UCP1, causing the mitochondrial proton gradient to break down and disrupting ATP formation (respiration). Futile cycles - two metabolic processes operating simultaneously in opposite directions - are active after the thermogenic activation of brown adipocytes. Free fatty acids are released, taken up again, and used to reform triglycerides, which are then broken down again. Other futile cycles are also active, such as the calcium cycle, the creatine cycle, the glycerol-3-phosphate cycle, the glycolysis-gluconeogenesis cycle, and potentially also a protein degradation and -synthesis cycle. These cycles use a lot of chemical energy in the form of ATP (adenosine triphosphate), which generates heat.
Despite significant advances in understanding the basic mechanisms of thermogenic lipolysis, substantial knowledge gaps remain regarding the precise molecular networks, temporal dynamics, and regulatory hierarchies that govern this complex process. Multi-omics approaches can help fill these knowledge gaps by providing systems-level insights that single-omics studies cannot achieve, particularly in understanding how genomic variations influence transcriptional programs, how these translate to proteomic changes, and how metabolic outputs reflect the integrated cellular response. By integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data, multi-omics can reveal previously hidden regulatory networks, identify biomarkers of thermogenic activation, and uncover the coordinated molecular changes that occur across different cellular compartments and time scales.
This study aims to investigate the molecular adaptation of immortalized murine brown adipocytes to thermogenic lipolysis by conducting a time-series experiment and multi-omics analysis. Specifically, we (1) collected transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data across key time points following norepinephrine stimulation, (2) applied various multi-omics integration methods to identify coordinated cellular responses and regulatory mechanisms underlying this metabolic adaptation, and (3) compared different multi-omics approaches to evaluate their strengths and limitations in capturing system-wide adaptations.
