ABSTRACT:
The development of ultrasensitive proteomic methods for the detection of proteins — potential tumor biomarkers — remains one of the key challenges in modern biomedicine. Based on data obtained using high-sensitivity technologies and the integration of proteomic and metabolomic layers of genome-encoded information, an expanded list of proteins associated with ovarian cancer progression has been compiled. A panel of ovarian cancer–specific markers, previously unreported in the scientific literature, has been identified. A total of 371 proteins were found to be potentially involved in the pathological process, with 33%  detected exclusively by ultrasensitive methods, and 26% discovered through metabolomic associations. Among these, 6% correspond to proteins already recognized as ovarian cancer–specific. Nine potential biomarkers are discovered for the first time, including immunoglobulin variable region proteins (IGLV7-46, IGHV3-15, IGLV2-18, IGHV1-2), as well as ATRN, CPN1, APOF, TGM3, and CRNN, which participate in diverse biological processes such as cellular transport, enzymatic activity, lipid metabolism, and the maintenance of tissue structural integrity. The identified biomarkers are present in blood at concentrations ranging from 10⁻¹² to 10⁻⁶ mol/L. The proposed approach overcomes the sensitivity limitations of conventional proteomic methods and may be adapted for the discovery of candidate markers in other multifactorial diseases.

PROJECT DESCRIPTION:
Advancing Plasma Proteomics for the Discovery of Ovarian Cancer Biomarkers 

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
Panoramic proteomic analysis was conducted in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Scientific, USA) coupled to a high-resolution Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The study cohort included plasma samples from 28 ovarian cancer patients (stages I–III) and 26 not pathological donors. Mass spectrometry data were processed using the MaxQuant software suite. A detailed description of all experimental procedures—including sample preparation, chromatographic conditions, mass spectrometry parameters, and data processing algorithms—is provided in Supplementary File 1 (Experimental Procedures). The sample preparation and DDA-Shotgun analysis protocol was based on a previously published method

DATA PROCESSING:
The raw data generated by all methods employed in this study are presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S3. Lists of core proteins and metabolite-associated proteins (PMconv) are provided in Supplementary Table S5. Data analysis involved processing raw data using Python (version 3.13.5) and the libraries pandas, numpy, matplotlib, seaborn, and scipy. Statistical significance of differential protein expression was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. To combine p-values obtained from different proteomic methods (DDA-Shotgun LC-MS/MS, SRM, AFM-MS), Fisher’s method was employed, allowing the aggregation of independent statistical assessments into a single significance metric despite differences in sample sets. This approach increases analytical power through cross-method convergence while maintaining non-pathological over type I errors (false discovery rate, FDR < 0.05, adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure). For visualization, Venn diagrams (using the venn Python library) and volcano plots were generated. Point colours indicated the proteomic methods in which proteins exhibited significant changes (FDR < 0.05, |log2 fold change| > 1). To analyse literature data on the identified candidate biomarkers, several resources were utilised, including DisGeNET 19, CancerMine 20, Open Targets 21, as well as data from a previously published study by Kliuchnikova et al. 8. To estimate theoretical intracellular protein concentrations (Figure 3c), data were extracted from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/, accessed 23 June 2025), specifically from the Plasma protein concentrations section. Protein lists identified by each proteomic method in patient samples were obtained, and concentrations reported in ng/mL were converted to molar units (mol/L) based on molecular weights retrieved from UniProt (Supporting Information: Table S4). These data formed the basis for comparative analysis of the concentration ranges detected by different methods and were used to construct the distributions shown in Figure 3c. Calculation of molar concentrations enabled evaluation of each method’s sensitivity to low-abundance proteins, which is critical for early diagnostic applications. Frequency comparison of protein occurrences between groups was performed using Fisher’s exact test, which is robust to sample size variations and reliable at low detection frequencies. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, with a threshold of FDR < 0.05.
