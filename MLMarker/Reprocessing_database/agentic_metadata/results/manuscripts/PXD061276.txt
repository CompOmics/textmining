ABSTRACT:
The SUMO pathway mainly functions to repress innate immunity in myeloid cells. Inactivating sumoylation triggers a strong, noncanonical, type I interferon (IFN1) response, amplified and coupled with inflammation upon stimulation. These findings transposed to pre-clinical models with the demonstration that sumoylation inhibitors activate antitumor immunity in an IFN1-dependent manner. Yet, how sumoylation represses immune signaling remains largely unknown. Here, we identified MORC3, a negative regulator of IFNB1, as the top SUMO2/3 substrate in myeloid cells. We show that, in monocytes, SUMO functions to repress basal IFNB1 in cis through a single long tandem repeat regulated by MORC3 [MORC3-regulated element (MRE)] that concentrates multiple motifs for the myeloid-enriched PU.1 factor. Inhibiting sumoylation induces a 3D genome reorganization nucleated from the MRE, which acquires both insulator and PU.1-activated enhancer activities, together with loss of H3.3 and H3K9me3 repressive marks and recruitment of PU.1. Paradoxically, MORC3, that interacts with PU.1, is massively recruited, yet unable to repress the MRE. Finally, we show that both sumoylation and MORC3 ATPase cycle are critical for MORC3 repressive activity. Our study thus uncovers an unconventional mechanism in which sumoylation, in concert with MORC3, orchestrates a metastable H3.3/H3K9me3 heterochromatin state on a multi-PU.1 binding platform to prevent an uncontrolled myeloid-specific immune response.

PROJECT DESCRIPTION:
SUMO operates from a unique long tandem repeat to keep innate immunity in check

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
Murine RAW 264.7 macrophage cells, which were either unstimulated or stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 1h or polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (Poly(I:C)) for 2h, respectively mimicking a bacterial or viral infection, were prepared. Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, prior to vigorous lysis using Guanidine Lysis Buffer (6M guanidine-HCl, 50 mM Tris pH 8.5). Lysates were snap frozen and stored at −80 °C until further processing, after which SUMOylated peptides were purified and analyzed by mass spectrometry essentially as described previously (Hendriks et al., 2018). Specifically, lysates were thawed at room temperature, and proteins reduced and alkylated using 5 mM chloroacetamide (CAA) and 5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP). Homogenization was performed via sonication, after which samples were centrifuged at 4,250g for 30 min and transferred to new tubes. An initial round of digestion was performed using 1:200 enzyme-to-protein ratio of endoproteinase Lys-C (Wako), overnight at room temperature. Samples were diluted 4-fold using 50 mM TRIS pH 8.5, after which a second round of digestion was done using 1:200 Lys-C, overnight at room temperature. Digests were acidified by addition of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 1%, and clarified from precipitate and debris by centrifugation at 4,250g at 4 °C for 30 min. Peptides were purified using 500 mg C8 Sep-Pak cartridges (Waters) following manufacturer’s instructions, with one cartridge used per 25 mg of digested protein. Pre-elution of small (irrelevant) peptides was done using 20% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% TFA, and elution of large (relevant) peptides was done using 5 mL of 45% ACN in 0.1% TFA. Elutions were frozen overnight at −80 °C, after which they were lyophilized over 96 h.

600 µL of Protein G Agarose beads (Roche) were used to immobilize 100 µL of SUMO-2/3 antibody (8A2, acquired from Abcam, ab81371; ~5-10 μg/μL antibody). Beads were pre-washed with PBS, after which antibody and beads were mixed in a 1.5 mL tube with ice-cold PBS added to maximum tube volume. Beads were incubated while mixing at 4 °C for 1 hour, washed three times with ice-cold PBS, after which antibodies were crosslinked to the beads via addition of 0.2 M sodium borate pH 9.0, freshly supplemented with 20 mM dimethyl pimelimidate (DMP). Crosslinking was done at room temperature for 30 min while keeping beads mixed, after which buffer was removed and the crosslinking step repeated. Following the 2nd crosslinking step, beads were washed two times with ice-cold PBS, twice with 0.1 M glycine pH 2.8, and three times with ice-cold PBS, prior to storage at 4 °C until usage.

Lyophilized peptides were dissolved in 10 mL ice-cold SUMO-IP buffer (50 mM MOPS, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 50 mM NaCl, buffered at pH 7.2), and clarified by centrifugation at 4,250g for 60 min at 4 °C. Samples were transferred to new tubes, after which 50 µL SUMO-IP beads (~8 µL of SUMO2/3 antibody) was added to each replicate sample. Incubation was performed for 2 h at 4 °C on a rotating mixer, followed by two washes with ice-cold SUMO-IP buffer, two washes with ice-cold PBS, and two washes with ice-cold ultrapure deionized water. Peptides were eluted using 100 µL of ice-cold 0.15% TFA, with beads standing on ice, gently mixed every 10 min, and a total elution time of 40 min. Elutions were cleared through 0.45 µm spin filters, and pH-neutralized via addition of 10 µL 1 M Na2HPO4. Subsequently, second-stage digestion was performed using 150 ng of Endoproteinase Asp-N (Roche), overnight at 30 °C, mixing at 300 rpm.

Quad-layer StageTips were prepared using four punch-outs of C18 material (Sigma-Aldrich, Empore™ SPE Disks, C18, 47 mm). StageTips were equilibrated using 100 μL of methanol, 100 μL of 80% ACN in 200 mM ammonium hydroxide, and two times 100 μL 50 mM ammonium hydroxide. Samples were basified by addition of 50 µL 200 mM ammonium hydroxide, and centrifuged at 14,000g for 2 min, just prior to loading them on StageTip. Loaded StageTips were washed twice with 100 μL 50 mM ammonium hydroxide, after which peptides were eluted as six fractions (F1-6) using 40 μL of 4, 7, 10, 13, 17, and 25% ACN in 50 mM ammonium hydroxide, and transferred to LoBind tubes. Fractions were dried to completion, using a vacuum concentrator for 2 h at 60°C, after which the dried peptides were dissolved using 10 μL of 0.1% formic acid and stored at −20 °C until analysis by MS.

All samples were analyzed on EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo). For each run, 5 μL of sample was injected. Separation of peptides was performed using 20-cm columns (75 μm internal diameter) packed in-house with ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ 1.9 µm beads (Dr. Maisch). Elution of peptides from the column was achieved using a gradient ranging from buffer A (0.1% formic acid) to buffer B (80% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid), at a flow of 250 nL/min. Gradient length was 80 min per sample, including ramp-up and wash-out, and an analytical gradient of 50 min. The buffer B ramp for the analytical gradient was as follows: F1: 13-24%, F2: 14-27%, F3-5: 15-30%, F6: 17-32%. The column was heated to 40°C using a column oven (Sonation), and ionization was achieved using a NanoSpray Flex™ NG ion source (Thermo). Spray voltage set at 2,000 V, ion transfer tube temperature to 275°C, and RF funnel level to 40%. Full scan range was set to 500-1,500 m/z, MS1 resolution to 120,000, MS1 AGC target to “200” (2,000,000 charges), and MS1 maximum injection time to “Auto”. Data was acquired in data-dependent mode, and the top 7 most abundant precursors were acquired each duty cycle. Precursors from a range of 550-1,500 m/z and with charge states 2-6 were selected using an isolation width of 1.3 m/z, and fragmented using higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with normalized collision energy of 25. Monoisotopic Precursor Selection (MIPS) was enabled in “Peptide” mode. Precursors were prevented from being repeatedly sequenced by setting expected peak width to 30 s, and setting dynamic exclusion duration to 60 s, with an exclusion mass tolerance of 15 ppm and exclusion of isotopes. MS/MS resolution was set to 60,000, MS/MS AGC target to “200” (200,000 charges), MS/MS intensity threshold to 170,000 charges/second, and MS/MS maximum injection time to “Auto”.


DATA PROCESSING:
All MS RAW data was analyzed using the freely available MaxQuant software, version 1.5.3.30 (Cox and Mann, 2008). All data was processed in a single computational run, and default MaxQuant settings were used, with exceptions specified below. For generation of the theoretical spectral library, the mouse FASTA database was downloaded from Uniprot on the 14th of February, 2020. The mature sequence of SUMO2 was inserted in the database to allow for detection of free SUMO. In silico digestion of theoretical peptides was performed with Lys-C, Asp-N, and Glu-N, allowing up to 8 missed cleavages. Variable modifications used were protein N-terminal acetylation, methionine oxidation, peptide N-terminal pyroglutamate, Ser/Thr/Tyr phosphorylation (STY), and Lys SUMOylation, with a maximum of 3 modifications per peptide. The SUMO mass remnant was defined as described previously (Hendriks et al., 2018); DVFQQQTGG, H60C41N12O15, monoisotopic mass 960.4301, neutral loss b7-DVFQQQT, diagnostic mass remnants [b2-DV, b3-DVF, b4-DVFQ, b5-DVFQQ, b6-DVFQQQ, b7-DVFQQQT, b9-DVFQQQTGG, QQ, FQ, FQQ]. Label-free quantification was enabled, with “Fast LFQ” disabled. Maximum peptide mass was set to 6,000 Da. Stringent MaxQuant 1% FDR filtering was applied (default), and additional automatic filtering was ensured by setting the minimum delta score for modified peptides to 20, with a site decoy fraction of 2%. Matching between runs was enabled, with a match time window of 1 min and an alignment window of 20 min. For protein quantification, the same variable modifications were included as for the peptide search. To further minimize false-positive discovery, additional manual filtering was performed at the peptide level. All modified peptides were required to have a localization probability of >75%, be supported by diagnostic mass remnants, be absent in the decoy database, and have a delta score of >40 in case SUMO modification was detected on a peptide C-terminal lysine not preceding an aspartic acid or glutamic acid. All multiply-SUMOylated peptides were discarded, unless the corresponding SUMO sites were also identified by singly-SUMOylated peptides. SUMO target proteins were derived from the “proteinGroups.txt” file, and all post-filtering SUMO sites were manually mapped. Only proteins containing at least one SUMO site were considered as SUMO target proteins, and other putative SUMO target proteins were discarded.

METHODS:
Materials and methods
Mouse cell lines
RAW 264.7 mouse macrophages were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) + Glutamax (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin, and passaged 1 in 10 every 2–3 days. When indicated, cells were treated with 10 ng/ml lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or with 20 μg/ml polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)], and the SUMOi ML-792 (MedChemExpress) at a final concentration of 2.5 μM.
Pre-B C10 cells (gift from T. Graf) were maintained in suspension at ∼106 cells per ml in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium + Glutamax (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin/streptomycin, 25 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 5 μM β-mercaptoethanol. When indicated, cells were treated with the SUMOi ML-792 (MedChemExpress) at a final concentration of 2.5 μM.
EL4 mouse T lymphoblasts were grown in complete RPMI 1640 (Life Technologies) containing glutamax and supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 ng/ml streptomycin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, and 5 μM β-mercaptoethanol (complete RPMI).
Human cell lines
THP1 human monocytes and were maintained in suspension at ∼106 cells per ml in RPMI + Glutamax (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin/streptomycin, 25 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 5 μM β-mercaptoethanol.
For THP1 macrophage differentiation, cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Abcam) to a final concentration of 50 nM.
Wild-type (WT), STAT1-/-STAT2-/-, STAT1-/-STAT2-/-ΔMRE, IFNAR1-/- IFNAR2-/-, IFNAR1-/- IFNAR2-/-MORC3-/-, PML-/- BlaER1 cell lines were previously described. They were maintained in suspension at ∼106 cells per ml in RPMI + Glutamax (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin/streptomycin, 25 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 5 μM β-mercaptoethanol.
HT-1080 (gift from E. Kandel) and HEK293T cells were grown in DMEM + Glutamax (Gibco) medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were passaged every 2 days and diluted one in six.
When indicated, cells were treated with TAK-981 (SUMOi, MedChemExpress) at a final concentration of 100 nM (for BlaER1) or 500 nM (for THP1 or HEK293T), and with 100 ng/ml LPS.
BlaER1 B lymphocyte to monocyte transdifferentiation
Human BlaER1 cells were transdifferentiated as described. Briefly, 0.5 × 106 cells per ml were seeded in 96-well plates in complete RPMI medium supplemented with 10 ng/ml hrIL-3 (Peprotech), 10 ng/ml hrM-CSF (Peprotech), and 100 nM β-estradiol (Sigma).
Sumoylome sample preparation
RAW 264.7 mouse macrophages were treated with 10 ng/ml LPS for 1 h, with 20 μg/ml poly(I:C) for 2 h, or left untreated. A total of 250 × 106 cells were used for each replicate, for each condition.
Cells were washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), prior to vigorous lysis using Guanidine Lysis Buffer (6 M guanidine–HCl, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.5). Lysates were snap frozen and stored at −80°C until further processing, after which sumoylated peptides were purified and analyzed by mass spectrometry essentially as described previously. Specifically, lysates were thawed at room temperature, and proteins reduced and alkylated using 5 mM chloroacetamide and 5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine. Homogenization was performed via sonication, after which samples were centrifuged at 4250 × g for 30 min and transferred to new tubes. An initial round of digestion was performed using 1:200 enzyme-to-protein ratio of endoproteinase Lys-C (Wako), overnight at room temperature. Samples were diluted four-fold using 50 mM Tris, pH 8.5, after which a second round of digestion was done using 1:200 Lys-C, overnight at room temperature. Digests were acidified by addition of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to a final concentration of 1%, and clarified from precipitate and debris by centrifugation at 4250 × g at 4°C for 30 min. Peptides were purified using 500 mg C8 Sep-Pak cartridges (Waters) following manufacturer’s instructions, with one cartridge used per 25 mg of digested protein. Pre-elution of small (irrelevant) peptides was done using 20% acetonitrile (ACN) in 0.1% TFA, and elution of large (relevant) peptides was done using 5 ml of 45% ACN in 0.1% TFA. Elutions were frozen overnight at −80°C, after which they were lyophilized over 96 h.
SUMO-IP bead preparation
Six hundred microliters of Protein G Agarose beads (Roche) were used to immobilize 100 μl of SUMO-2/3 antibody (8A2, acquired from Abcam, ab81371; ∼5–10-μg/μl antibody). Beads were pre-washed with PBS, after which antibody and beads were mixed in a 1.5-ml tube with ice-cold PBS added to maximum tube volume. Beads were incubated while mixing at 4°C for 1 h, washed three times with ice-cold PBS, after which antibodies were crosslinked to the beads via addition of 0.2 M sodium borate, pH 9.0, freshly supplemented with 20 mM dimethyl pimelimidate. Crosslinking was done at room temperature for 30 min while keeping beads mixed, after which buffer was removed and the crosslinking step repeated. Following the secondcrosslinking step, beads were washed two times with ice-cold PBS, twice with 0.1 M glycine, pH 2.8, and three times with ice-cold PBS, prior to storage at 4°C until usage.
Purification of sumoylated peptides
Lyophilized peptides were dissolved in 10 ml ice-cold SUMO-IP buffer (50 mM MOPS, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 50 mM NaCl, buffered at pH 7.2), and clarified by centrifugation at 4250 × g for 60 min at 4°C. Samples were transferred to new tubes, after which 50 μl SUMO-IP beads (∼8 μl of SUMO2/3 antibody) was added to each replicate sample. Incubation was performed for 2 h at 4°C on a rotating mixer, followed by two washes with ice-cold SUMO-IP buffer, two washes with ice-cold PBS, and two washes with ice-cold ultrapure deionized water. Peptides were eluted using 100 μl of ice-cold 0.15% TFA, with beads standing on ice, gently mixed every 10 min, and a total elution time of 40 min. Elutions were cleared through 0.45-μm spin filters, and pH-neutralized via addition of 10 μl 1 M Na2HPO4. Subsequently, second-stage digestion was performed using 150 ng of Endoproteinase Asp-N (Roche), overnight at 30°C, mixing at 300 rpm.
On-StageTip high-pH fractionation
Quad-layer StageTips were prepared using four punch-outs of C18 material (Sigma–Aldrich, Empore™ SPE Disks, C18, 47 mm). StageTips were equilibrated using 100 μl of methanol, 100 μl of 80% ACN in 200 mM ammonium hydroxide, and two times 100 μl 50 mM ammonium hydroxide. Samples were basified by addition of 50 μl 200 mM ammonium hydroxide, and centrifuged at 14 000 × g for 2 min, just prior to loading them on StageTip. Loaded StageTips were washed twice with 100 μl 50-mM ammonium hydroxide, after which peptides were eluted as six fractions (F1-6) using 40 μl of 4%, 7%, 10%, 13%, 17%, and 25% ACN in 50 mM ammonium hydroxide, and transferred to LoBind tubes. Fractions were dried to completion, using a vacuum concentrator for 2 h at 60°C, after which the dried peptides were dissolved using 10 μl of 0.1% formic acid and stored at −20°C until analysis by mass spectrometry (MS).
MS analysis
All samples were analyzed on EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo). For each run, 5 μl of sample was injected. Separation of peptides was performed using 20-cm columns (75 μm internal diameter) packed in-house with ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ 1.9-μm beads (Dr Maisch). Elution of peptides from the column was achieved using a gradient ranging from buffer A (0.1% formic acid) to buffer B (80% ACN in 0.1% formic acid), at a flow of 250 nl/min. Gradient length was 80 min per sample, including ramp-up and wash-out, and an analytical gradient of 50 min. The buffer B ramp for the analytical gradient was as follows: F1: 13%–24%, F2: 14%–27%, F3-5: 15%–30%, F6: 17%–32%. The column was heated to 40°C using a column oven (Sonation), and ionization was achieved using a NanoSpray Flex™ NG ion source (Thermo). Spray voltage set at 2000 V, ion transfer tube temperature to 275°C, and ratio-to-front (RF) funnel level to 40%. Full scan range was set to 500–1500 m/z, MS1 resolution to 120 000, MS1 AGC target to “200” (2 000 000 charges), and MS1 maximum injection time to “Auto”. Data was acquired in data-dependent mode, and the top 7 most abundant precursors were acquired each duty cycle. Precursors from a range of 550–1500 m/z and with charge states 2–6 were selected using an isolation width of 1.3 m/z, and fragmented using higher-energy collision dissociation with normalized collision energy of 25. Monoisotopic Precursor Selection was enabled in “Peptide” mode. Precursors were prevented from being repeatedly sequenced by setting expected peak width to 30 s, and setting dynamic exclusion duration to 60 s, with an exclusion mass tolerance of 15 ppm and exclusion of isotopes. MS/MS resolution was set to 60 000, MS/MS AGC target to “200” (200 000 charges), MS/MS intensity threshold to 170 000 charges s−1, and MS/MS maximum injection time to “Auto”.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting
For fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis, cells were incubated with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Scientific) in PBS for 10 min to mark dead cells, and with PE conjugated anti-CD11b and APC conjugated anti-CD19 (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were counted on a BD FACSAria III Cell Sorter, and data was analyzed using FlowJo software.
Co-immunoprecipitation
Cells were washed and harvested by scraping in ice-cold PBS freshly supplemented with 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM; from 500 mM stock in ethanol; Sigma). Cells were lysed in 1× Chris buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% (v/v) Igepal CA-630 (Sigma I8896), 10% (v/v) glycerol, and freshly added cOmplete Protease Inhibitor cocktail (1 tablet/50 ml; Roche) buffer, PhosSTOP cocktail (1 tablet/10 ml; Roche), and 10 mM NEM] and briefly sonicated. Extracts were clarified by centrifugation (15 000 rpm/10 min.), 0.22-μm filtration and further pre-cleared by 30 min. incubation with 1/50th volume of equilibrated Sephadex G-25 beads. Antibodies (2 μg), against PU.1 (Santa Cruz sc-390405) and FLAG-M2 (Sigma F1804), or normal IgG control (2 μg; Millipore 12-371) were added and incubated for >4 h before adding 50 μl slurry of pre-equilibrated Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific Ref. 26162) for overnight incubation. Beads were washed three times 5 min. in 1 ml lysis buffer and eluted with 30 μl 1.25× Laemmli buffer [5× stock: 313 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 10% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 50% (v/v) glycerol, 25% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, 0.01% (w/v) bromophenol Blue] and electrophoresed for immunoblotting together with aliquots of total extract (Input) and “flow-through” extracts remaining after antibody/bead incubation. Where indicated, treatment of PU.1 immunoprecipitates was carried out with RNAseA (Qiagen, 1 μg per reaction) plus dsDNAse (Thermo EN0771; 2 μl per reaction for 1 h at 37°C, including a spike-in of 5 μg pGEM7 (Promega) plasmid to determine digestion efficacy by subsequent bacterial transformation of an aliquot after incubation), ethidium bromide (Euromedex EU0070; 100 μg per immunoprecipitation) and recombinant SENP2 de-SUMOylase (expressed and purified in-house from BL21(DE3) cells by standard methods as a GST fusion of the SENP2 catalytic domain; 5 μg per reaction, 1 h at 37°C).
Immunoblots
Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed directly in 1.25× Laemmli buffer. After 5 min incubation at 95°C, cells were sonicated twice for 15 s, then centrifugated at full speed for 5 min. Total proteins were quantified using Pierce solution supplemented with Ionic Detergent Compatibility Reagent (Thermo Scientific) following manufacturer’s instructions. Equal quantities of proteins were resolved on 4%–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis Bis–Tris gels (Bio-Rad), then transferred on a 0.22-μm nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies against MORC3 (Rockland 100-401-N96), GAPDH (Abcam ab8245), Dmap1 (Santa Cruz sc-373949), PU.1 (Santa Cruz sc-390405), and Actin (Sigma ZRB1312 and Sigma A1978) were incubated with the membrane overnight at 4°C. Secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h (LiCor: 926-32211, 926-32210, 926-68021, 926-68020). Membranes were analyzed on an Odyssey scanner (LiCor), and TIFF images exported with ImageStudio (Licor).
Quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was extracted using 1 ml Trizol reagent (Thermo Scientific 15596026) and 100 μl 1-bromo-3-chloropropane (BCP; Thermo Scientific 106860501). The upper phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated using 2-propanol, washed with 80% ethanol, resuspended in water, and quantified. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using 1 μg RNA and a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) and diluted in 200 μl water.
Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using SybR green mix and the indicated primers (Supplementary Table S7), using cDNA for messenger RNA (mRNA) quantification or genomic DNA for local chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR). Of note, the MRE primer pair for local ChIP-qPCR amplifies a region located 100 bp upstream of the MRE. Indeed, owing to the repetitive nature of the MRE, primers designed in the MRE produce amplicons of multiple sizes, thus rendering them unsuitable for quantitative PCR. The change-in-cycling-threshold (ΔCt) values were calculated relative to Ct values from the amplification of endogenous GAPDH mRNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a CFX96 PCR system (Bio-Rad).
RNA-seq
Total RNA was extracted using 1 ml Trizol reagent and 100 μl BCP. The upper phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated using 2-propanol, washed with 80% ethanol, resuspended in water, and quantified.
For bulk RNA-sequencing, RNA samples were analyzed with a TapeStation (Agilent) and further processed if the RNA integrity number was superior to 8.
RNA concentration was measured with a Qubit (Invitrogen). The library preparation and sequencing were done at IGBMC (Illkirch, France). Total RNA-seq libraries were generated using Library prep stranded total RNA Ribozero Plus Ligation (Illumina) following manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq2000-X sequencer as paired end 50 base reads, following Illumina’s instructions.
RNA-seq analysis
Reads were mapped with the STAR aligner program on hg38 genome and the gencode annotation v34 with the ENCODE parameters: –runMode alignReads –outFilterType BySJout –outFilterMultimapNmax 20 –alignSJoverhangMin 8 –alignSJDBoverhangMin 1 –outFilterMismatchNmax 999 –outFilterMismatchNoverReadLmax 0.04 –alignIntronMin 20 –alignIntronMax 1 000 000 –alignMatesGapMax 1 000 000 –outSAMstrandField intronMotif
Alignments were filtered with Samtools on MAPQ30. Counts matrix was generated with the featuresCount program from the subread package. Differential expression analysis was conducted with the DESeq2 R package.
ChIP-seq
Cells were collected and fixed in 1% formaldehyde (Thermo Scientific) for 10 min at room temperature. The reaction was quenched using glycine at a final concentration of 125 mM for 5 min. Cells were washed twice in ice-cold PBS.
For fragmentation by sonication, cells were lysed in nuclei extraction buffer [250 mM sucrose, 10 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)] for 10 min on ice. The extracted nuclei were resuspended in nuclei lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic (EDTA) acid, 0.2% SDS, and cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)], and sonicated with a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode) using six cycles (30 s on, 30 s off), until chromatin fragments reached a size of around 300 base pairs (bp).
For fragmentation by Micrococcal Nuclease (Mnase) digestion, cells were lysed with SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Cell Lysis Buffers A and B (Cell Signaling) following manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were incubated with Mnase (Cell Signaling) for 20 min at 37°C, until the chromatin fragments were digested to the size of 150–450 bp.
After fragmentation, the size of the chromatin fragments was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Chromatin was incubated overnight with the following antibodies: anti-MORC3 (Rockland 100-401-N97), anti-Morc3 (Rockland 100-401-N96), anti-PU.1 (Cell Signaling 2266S), anti-SUMO2/3 (Abcam ab3742), anti-H3K9me3 (Abcam ab8898), anti-H3.3 (Abcam ab176840), anti-H3K27ac (Diagenode C15410196), anti-H3K4me3 (Active Motif 39159), anti-CTCF (Active Motif 61932), anti-H3K27me3 (Millipore 07-449), and anti-H2A.Z (Abcam ab4174). Transcription factor sites analysis of the MRE
Transcription factor (TF) binding sites in the MRE region were analyzed using the MATCH algorithm in conjunction with the TRANSFAC® library, release 2024.1. The analysis was done on the geneXplain bioinformatics platform (https://genexplain.com/transfac-product/#geneXplain-platform). We initially filtered the full TRANSFAC library of positional weight matrices and selected 2816 motifs corresponding to TFs expressed in monocytes and macrophages. This filtering step ensured the relevance of identified motifs to cell types involved in immune responses. Using the MATCH algorithm, we analyzed motif enrichment within the MRE repeats (Yes set) in comparison to 10 000 randomly selected noncoding genomic regions of the same length (200 bp) (No set). Enrichment was assessed using two measures: (i) Site enrichment score: The ratio of the frequency of motif occurrences in the Yes set relative to the No set; and (ii) Sequence enrichment score: The ratio of the number of sequences in the Yes set containing at least one occurrence of a given motif to the number of such sequences in the No set. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied to compute the FDR associated with the enrichment scores. As a result, 433 motifs exhibiting significant enrichment were identified, with a site FDR <10−8 and a sequence FDR <0.05. These motifs corresponded to 80 distinct TF families. For each TF family, we selected one representative motif with the lowest site FDR (Supplementary Table S4). To further refine our analysis, we ranked the enriched SPI1/PU.1 and IRF motifs based on their enrichment scores (Supplementary Table S5). We selected the top-ranking motifs from each TF family (V$SPI1_06 and V$IRF2_01). Additionally, we selected V$IRF3_06 to complement V$IRF2_01, ensuring comprehensive motif representation across all MRE repeat.

Results
Mapping the endogenous sumoylome in the context of innate immunity
To investigate the role of SUMO in the innate immune system, we used large-scale proteomics to identify the set of endogenous SUMO2/3 substrates in myeloid cells and their sumoylation dynamics upon innate immune stimulation. As a model system, we used murine RAW 264.7 macrophage cells, which were either unstimulated or stimulated with LPS or poly(I:C), mimicking a bacterial or viral infection, respectively (Fig. 1A). Altogether, we identified 3630 SUMO2/3 sites, mapping to 1232 SUMO2/3 target proteins (Fig. 1B and C, Supplementary Fig. S1A–C, and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Most (∼89%) SUMO2/3 substrates were found to be nuclear proteins (Supplementary Fig. S1D) and, among them, the epigenetic regulator Morc3 ranked as the top hit (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Table S2).
We next assessed whether immune stimulation induced changes in the sumoylation levels of SUMO2/3 targets. While the vast majority of the sumoylome remained unchanged after LPS or poly(I:C) treatment, we observed 57 or 44 significantly dynamic SUMO2/3 protein substrates (Fig. 1E and F), respectively. Although LPS treatment induced a somewhat stronger shift in the sumoylome, both treatments resulted in similar SUMO2/3 dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S1E), with a strong Pearson correlation (R= 0.58) between LPS and poly(I:C) ratios, suggesting that the overall SUMO2/3 immune response is comparable between both stimuli. Among the 85 proteins significantly regulated by either of the two treatments, 37 showed increased sumoylation [26 for LPS, 22 for poly(I:C), 11 being common] and 48 exhibited reduced levels of SUMO2/3 modification [31 for LPS, 22 for poly(I:C), 5 being common substrates] (Fig. 1E and F and Supplementary Table S2). Most (∼79%) dynamic SUMO2/3 targets belonged to a functionally interconnected cluster enriched for proteins involved in early growth response, chromatin regulation and chromosome transactions (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S1F). The changes in the sumoylation status of the 57 LPS-induced dynamic proteins were not due to similar changes in overall protein abundance, except for five of them, Egr1, Nfkbiz, Fos, Maff, and Junb, all of which showed both increased modification and protein levels and correspond to well-known early response proteins (Supplementary Fig. S1G and Supplementary Table S2). Most interestingly, among the proteins found to be hyposumoylated upon LPS, we found three subunits of the Tip60 histone acetyltransferase complex, Dmap1, Trrap, and Ruvbl1 (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Table S2). Notably, the sumoylation levels of these proteins were not altered by poly(I:C) treatment. Inspection of our dataset revealed 15 out of 18 subunits of the Tip60 complex to be SUMO2/3 target proteins (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Table S2). Analyzing the relative modification ratio of the individual Tip60 subunit substrates revealed two additional proteins, Ep400 and Ruvbl2, that are involved in the Tip60-mediated H2A.Z loading activity, to be significantly hyposumoylated following LPS treatment (Fig. 1H). Moreover, across the sumoylated proteins of the Tip60 complex, 14 (∼93%) Tip60 subunits showed a downtrend in sumoylation, and as a whole, the Tip60 complex was significantly less sumoylated in response to LPS (Fig. 1H and I). We could confirm sumoylation of the Dmap1 subunit by immunoblotting and the decrease in sumoylation levels as early as 30 min following LPS stimulation, which subsequently recovered at a later stage (Supplementary Fig. S1H).
Taken together, this demonstrates that the Tip60 complex is highly sumoylated under normal conditions, and that this sumoylation is partially lost upon LPS activation, suggesting a role for dynamic sumoylation of Tip60 in myeloid cell activation.
Sumoylation inhibition phenocopies loss of MORC3 in basal IFNB1 expression regulation
To then gain insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the strong basal induction of IFNB1 triggered by loss of sumoylation in myeloid cells, we focused on MORC3 for the following reasons. First, Morc3 appeared as the most heavily SUMO2/3-modified substrate in murine macrophages that showed no dynamic sumoylation upon innate stimulation (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. S2A, and Supplementary Table S2). Second, loss of MORC3 in human monocytic cells, achieved either through genetic inactivation or degradation by the herpes simplex virus type 1 ubiquitin ligase ICP0, leads to a strong upregulation of IFNB1 in basal conditions. Finally, similar to SUMOi-mediated activation of IFN1 signaling, the response triggered by MORC3 depletion is independent of canonical IFN-inducing pathways and relies solely on IFNB1 de-repression.
We thus studied the possible crosstalk between SUMO and MORC3 in human monocytes using the efficient transdifferentiation BlaER1 system (Supplementary Fig. S2B). We first verified that MORC3 is heavily sumoylated in BlaER1 monocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2C) and confirmed the dominant IFN1 response induced by SUMOi in these cells by RNA-seq analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2D and Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, we found MORC3 to be more heavily sumoylated in monocytes compared to B lymphocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Comparative transcriptomic analysis of genes differentially expressed in MORC3-/- and SUMOi-treated monocytes in IFNAR1-/-IFNAR2-/- (henceforth referred to as IFNAR-/-) genetic background revealed that IFNB1 was, by far, the most strongly upregulated gene in both settings relative to control conditions (Fig. 2A). We next assessed IFNB1 expression levels in a time series of transdifferentiation states from BlaER1 B lymphocytes to monocytes. Given that continuous SUMOi treatment leads to cell death, cells were treated with TAK-981 for 24 h at different days of the transdifferentiation process prior to mRNA extraction. Although SUMOi failed to activate IFNB1 in B lymphocytes, a potent induction was observed as cells started to transdifferentiate, which attained a plateau after 3 days (Fig. 2B). Unsurprisingly, withdrawal of SUMOi led to restoration of MORC3 sumoylation and almost complete IFNB1 repression after 48 h (Supplementary Fig. S2E). As expected, similar patterns of IFNB1 activation were observed in IFNAR-/- or STAT1-/-STAT2-/- genetic backgrounds along with the transdifferentiation process (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S2F). Increased basal IFNB1 expression was also visible upon SUMOi in human THP1 monocytes, RAW 264.7 macrophages and murine C10 monocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2G). Interestingly, follow up of IFNB1 activation during BlaER1 transdifferentiation in the absence of MORC3 showed similar IFNB1 regulation profiles as those observed for sequential SUMOi treatment (Fig. 2C). In addition, whereas loss of MORC3 was ∼5 fold more efficient than TAK-981 at de-repressing IFNB1, combination of both failed to reveal any synergistic or even additive effect (Fig. 2D), suggesting that MORC3 and SUMO function in the same IFNB1 repressive pathway.
MORC3 was reported to localize in antiviral promyelocytic leukemia (PML) nuclear bodies (NBs) which serve as sumoylation factories. Similar to what was observed in MORC3-/- monocytes in which the absence of PML and associated NBs did not affect the induction of IFNB1, SUMOi-induced IFNB1 activation requires neither PML nor its associated NBs (Fig. 2E), demonstrating that both MORC3 and SUMO regulate IFNB1 independently of these structures. Collectively, these data indicate that loss of either sumoylation or MORC3 results in a very similar IFNB1 transcriptional response and that the IFNB1 gene represents the major target for SUMO- and MORC3-mediated co-repression in myeloid cells.
Sumoylation and MORC3 ATPase cycle are required for proper IFNB1 repression
To then explore the direct role of sumoylation on MORC3 repressive properties, we performed rescue experiments. Dox-inducible re-expression of MORC3 at endogenous levels in MORC3-/- IFNAR-/- BlaER1 monocytes was able to restore most (89%) IFNB1 silencing (Fig. 3A and B). The fact that this repression is not complete, as observed following recovery from SUMOi (Supplementary Fig. S2E), suggests possible irreversible changes incurred by constitutive loss of MORC3 or SUMOi in the chromatin environment of the IFNB1 locus. Inhibiting global sumoylation blunted the repressive activity of exogenous MORC3 by 37%, restoring IFNB1 mRNA levels similar to those observed in MORC3-sufficient monocytes treated with TAK-981 only (Fig. 3B). This finding demonstrates that cellular sumoylation is required for full silencing properties of MORC3.
To address the role of MORC3 functional domains and MORC3 sumoylation in IFNB1 repression, we re-expressed a series of MORC3 mutants in MORC3-/-IFNAR-/- BlaER1 monocytes (Fig. 3A). Dimerization or dissociation of the MORC3 ATPase domain, which are dependent on ATP binding or hydrolysis, respectively, are expected to function as conformational switches important for MORC3 cellular function. The CW ligand binding mutant (W419A), which remains in an autoinhibited state, the ATP binding mutant (G101A) and the ATPase mutant (E35A) were as previously described (Fig. 3A). Our SUMO2/3 proteomics analysis identified 28 sumoylated lysine residues across the Morc3 protein (Supplementary Table S1), in line with the multiple sumoylated MORC3 species observed by western blotting (Supplementary Fig. S2A and C). We generated a MORC3 derivative harboring mutations of the four conserved lysine residues (K597R, K650R, K651R, and K794R) that recapitulate 91% of Morc3 sumoylation (Supplementary Table S1) and introduced another mutation (K740R) as reported previously. In contrast to what was observed in HeLa cells in which mutation of the five SUMO acceptor sites almost entirely abolished MORC3 sumoylation, the 5KR MORC3 mutant only showed a modest reduction in sumoylation when compared to its native counterpart in monocytes (Fig. 3A).
This feature is likely due to the ability of SUMO to modify other lysine residues after mutating the main acceptor sites of multisumoylated proteins, complicating efforts to generate fully SUMO-deficient mutants for these substrates. The W419A and G101A mutants were severely impaired for SUMO modification, indicating that both CW ligand-binding and ATP-binding activities are essential for efficient MORC3 modification (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the ATPase E35A mutant, which fails to de-dimerize, was not affected and rather displayed higher sumoylation levels, suggesting that MORC3 dimer formation favors MORC3 sumoylation. We then assessed the ability of the various MORC3 mutants to restore IFNB1 silencing when added back to MORC3-/-IFNAR-/- BlaER1 monocytes (Fig. 3C). The ATP binding, ATP hydrolysis and CW mutants were all found to be defective in their repressive activity when compared to exogenous WT MORC3.
When tested in similar conditions, the 5KR derivative, which is slightly (14%) hyposumoylated compared to exogenous WT MORC3 (Fig. 3A), showed a weak, though consistent, similar decrease (15%) in its repressive potential (Fig. 3C), for which, however, the possible effect of other lysine-targeting PTMs cannot be strictly ruled out. Collectively, these data demonstrate that the MORC3-ATPase cycle is needed to prevent spontaneous IFNB1 expression and that MORC3 sumoylation likely contributes to its repressive activity.
SUMO controls IFNB1 from a PU.1-activated/MORC3-regulated tandem repeat
MORC3 exerts its repressing activity through a MORC3-regulated element (MRE), located ∼100 kb downstream of IFNB1. We thus investigated the possible contribution of the MRE in SUMOi-triggered IFNB1 de-repression. The massive induction of IFNB1 transcripts observed in STAT1-/-STAT2-/- BlaER1 monocytes treated with TAK-981 was entirely abolished in the absence of the MRE (Fig. 4A). In contrast, IFNB1 activation upon LPS was MRE-independent, in agreement with previous studies. Thus, the MRE is required for SUMOi-mediated IFNB1 de-repression. A similar MRE requirement was also observed for SUMOi-induced expression of MLLT3, a neighboring gene that is co-regulated with IFNB1 by MORC3 (Fig. 4B). Of note, solely deleting the MRE leads to a weak de-repression of IFNB1 and MLLT3. We next assessed the role of the MRE on IFNB1 promoter activity in reporter assays using RAW 264.7 macrophages. The negative-control reporter, which contains an irrelevant 2.1-kb sequence upstream of the IFNB1 promoter alone, failed to respond to SUMOi, in agreement with an effect of SUMOi being mediated from outside the promoter (Fig. 4C). Although, in control conditions, the MRE readily conferred substantial activation to the reporter construct, treatment with SUMOi, but not with LPS, led to a sharp increase in MRE-driven transcriptional activity (Fig. 4C). Thus, the MRE is both necessary and sufficient to activate IFNB1 upon sumoylation inhibition, demonstrating that SUMO operates from this element to repress basal IFN1 signaling.
The MRE region (here called MRE-S), which is unique in the genome, includes some 10 well conserved repeats ∼190-bp long, each consisting of a tandem ∼95-bp sub-repeat (Supplementary Fig. S3A). A smaller repeated element of ∼1.5 kb occupies the syntenic region in mouse (Supplementary Fig. S3B). This element similarly stimulated Ifnb1 promoter-driven luciferase activity, that was further enhanced by SUMOi but not by LPS (Supplementary Fig. S3C). In human, we identified a longer second allele (MRE-L) characterized by insertion of two additional ∼190bp repeats (Supplementary Fig. S3D), that conferred slightly higher transcriptional responsiveness to SUMOi when compared to MRE-S (Supplementary Fig. S3E). Of note, both MRE-S and MRE-L alleles are present in BlaER1 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Analysis of a large number of human genomes should help in identifying other potential alleles harboring variable number of tandem repeats and exploring their possible association with any clinically relevant IFN-linked phenotypes.
The finding that B lymphocytes are unable to activate IFNB1 in response to SUMOi whereas monocytes are highly sensitive (Fig. 2B) led us to hypothesize that one or several transcriptional activator(s), specifically enriched in monocytes, mediate MRE-dependent IFNB1 induction in myeloid cells. Enrichment analysis for transcription factor-binding motifs in the MRE using TRANSFAC revealed PU.1, a major regulator of immune cell differentiation significantly enriched in myeloid cells, as well as several IRF family members, among the top scoring predicted motifs (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, one specific PU.1 motif was found to be repeated twice on average per ∼95-bp sub-repeat with frequent neighboring IRF motifs (Fig. 4D and E, and Supplementary Fig. S3A). The massive accumulation of PU.1 and, to a lesser extent, of IRF sites in the MRE (Supplementary Table S5) suggests that this tandem repeat behaves as a platform for extensive recruitment of numerous PU.1 and IRF factors when necessary. Concordantly, published ChIP-seq data in human macrophages and monocytes revealed accumulation of PU.1 and IRF1 at the MRE, suggesting a role for PU.1, possibly cooperating with one or several IRF members, in MRE regulation (Fig. 4F). In line with this, PU.1 was reported to cooperate with IRF8 on a composite PU.1-IRF element in the IFNB1 promoter for rapid induction of IFNB1 upon innate immune stimulation in monocytes. Interestingly, we show that inhibiting sumoylation induced recruitment of PU.1 at the MRE in BlaER1 monocytes, but not in B lymphocytes (Fig. 4G).
To next explore a possible direct contribution of PU.1 in SUMOi-driven IFNB1 activation, we overexpressed this factor in human fibrosarcoma HT-1080 cells which naturally do not express PU.1. While treatment with SUMOi alone showed no effect, PU.1 overexpression slightly induced IFNB1 expression (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. S3F), an effect likely due to IFNB1 promoter activation. Remarkably, inhibiting sumoylation strongly enhanced IFNB1 expression in PU.1-overexpressing cells but not in mock-transfected cells (Fig. 4H). These results indicate that solely expressing PU.1 in nonimmune cells is sufficient to trigger SUMOi-driven IFNB1 activation. To substantiate the role of PU.1 in MRE activation, we analyzed reporter gene activity in HT-1080 cells and in 293T cells that, similar to HT-1080 cells, lack PU.1 and in murine EL4 T lymphoblasts. While the MRE did not confer any transcriptional activity in control conditions, expression of PU.1 led to a sharp increase in transcriptional activity of the MRE reporter compared to the promoter alone in all three conditions (Fig. 4I and J and Supplementary Fig. S3G). Of note, unlike the strong inducing effect of SUMOi on PU.1-driven endogenous IFNB1 expression (Fig. 4H), such enhancing effect of SUMOi was not observed in the context of the MRE reporter in HT-1080 and 293T cells (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Fig. S3G). This difference is likely due to the absence of possible cooperating immune co-factors or the inappropriately chromatinized nature of the episomal reporter operating out of its native genomic context in nonimmune cells.
Together, these data demonstrate that SUMO controls IFNB1 activity from the MRE. The myeloid PU.1 factor, which is recruited upon SUMOi to the MRE that harbors multiple PU.1-binding sites, is able to both activate the MRE and to confer SUMOi responsiveness to the native IFNB1 gene in nonimmune cells.
Sumoylation regulates a metastable H3.3/H3K9me3 heterochromatin state on the MRE
The MRE is a key component of MORC3-mediated repression of IFNB1; however, whether MORC3 indeed binds the MRE remained unknown. To address this issue, we performed ChIP-seq experiments in IFNAR-/- BlaER1 monocytes with an antibody against MORC3. To ascertain specificity of the obtained signals, data were normalized to similar ChIP-seq experiments performed in MORC3-/-IFNAR-/- monocytes. While the vast majority of detected signals were present in both MORC3-sufficient and MORC3-deficient cells, we identified 52 MORC3-specific peaks, including the MRE, in control conditions (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S6). Such enrichment was not visible on the IFNB1 promoter (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Similar ChIP-seq experiments performed in IFNAR-sufficient BlaER1 monocytes and lymphocytes confirmed MORC3 enrichment on the MRE in monocytes, whereas lymphocytes showed very weak MORC3 signal, if any (Fig. 5A). Of note, unmappable reads corresponding to the repetitive central MRE region were excluded from the analysis. Unexpectedly, SUMOi treatment of BlaER1 monocytes, although inducing strong IFNB1 activation, led to a massive recruitment of MORC3 on the MRE, suggesting the accumulation of repression-incompetent MORC3 on this element in sumoylation-depleted monocytes (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). Similar recruitment was observed at only two of the other MORC3-specific peaks indicating that such accumulation is not a general consequence of sumoylation inhibition (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table S6). Remarkably, these two peaks, similar to what was observed at the MRE, overlap with tandem repeats at the AFG3L1P and ERICH1 loci (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S4B). Similar findings were obtained in THP1 monocytes in which treatment with SUMOi increased MORC3 occupancy at the MRE as well as at the AFG3L1P and ERICH1 loci (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Intriguingly, such SUMOi-induced recruitment of MORC3 to the MRE was not visible in BlaER1 lymphocytes which fail to upregulate IFNB1 expression (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Given that PU.1 is recruited to the MRE upon SUMOi in BlaER1 monocytes (Fig. 4G), we tested the possibility that PU.1 may interact with MORC3. Co-immunoprecipitation assays carried out in 293T cells overexpressing both PU.1 and MORC3 or PU.1 and MORC3-FLAG revealed that PU.1 could pull down MORC3 in a sumoylation-independent manner and reciprocally, that FLAG-tagged MORC3 could pull down PU.1 (Fig. 5C). The same experiment in 293T cells overexpressing the MORC3 mutants revealed that the interaction is independent of the MORC3 ATPase cycle (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Treatment of PU.1 immunoprecipitates with DNAse/RNAseA or ethidium bromide did not disrupt the protein interaction, indicating that it is also DNA/RNA independent (Supplementary Fig. S4E). These data suggest that, as a counter-acting process, MORC3, likely recruited by PU.1, accumulates at the MRE in monocytes, though it fails to silence IFNB1 expression.
Analysis of genome-wide SUMO2/3 distribution in IFNAR-/- BlaER1 cells revealed a large SUMO2/3 peak across the MRE in monocytes (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). This peak was retained on the MRE in the absence of MORC3, suggesting that MORC3 does not constitute the most abundant SUMO substrate on this element or that MORC3 is replaced by other heavily sumoylated target(s) when it is lost (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). High levels of SUMO2/3 were similarly associated with the two MORC3-enriched tandem repeats at the AFG3L1P and ERICH1 loci (Fig. 5B). ChIP-seq profiling in murine RAW 264.7 macrophages also showed SUMO2/3 peaking together with Morc3 on the MRE (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Interestingly, Trim33 (Tif1γ), another heavily sumoylated substrate (Fig. 1D) that, like Morc3, represses Ifnb1 expression was similarly strongly enriched on the MRE in RAW 264.7 cells (Supplementary Fig. S4F).
We then evaluated the repressive chromatin state at the MRE. In ESCs, sumoylation of MORC3 was reported to be critical for its interaction with DAXX and subsequent H3.3 incorporation at endogenous retroviral elements (ERVs). Moreover, sumoylation plays an important role in heterochromatin formation involving both KAP1/SETDB1- and SUV39H1-mediated H3K9 trimethylation. To investigate the chromatin patterns of H3.3 and H3K9me3 at the MRE and their possible dynamics, we performed ChIP-seq experiments in BlaER1 monocytes with or without SUMOi treatment. We found both H3.3 and H3K9me3 to be prominently enriched on the MRE and strongly reduced in the absence of sumoylation (Fig. 5D and E and Supplementary Fig. S4G). Loss of MORC3 induced a similar decrease in H3K9me3 levels in IFNAR-deficient BlaER1 monocytes (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, both repressive marks were also present at the MORC3-bound tandem repeats in the AFG3L1P and ERICH1 loci and decreased following loss of sumoylation (Fig. 5B). Conversely, levels of H3K27ac, a mark characteristic of active enhancers, increased at the MRE upon SUMOi treatment (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, in contrast to monocytes, H3K9me3 and H3.3 levels on the MRE were low and stable in BlaER1 B lymphocytes (Fig. 5D and E), indicating that a robust sumoylation-dependent repressive chromatin environment installs on the MRE in highly SUMOi-responsive monocytes. Similar findings were obtained in mouse where H3.3 and H3K9me3 were found to be enriched at the MRE in RAW 264.7 macrophages and lost upon sumoylation inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S4F).
Together, these data demonstrate that both MORC3 and SUMO2/3 function through direct binding on the MRE to silence basal IFNB1 expression and suggest that SUMOi-induced loss of H3.3 and H3K9me3 heterochromatin, together with PU.1 recruitment, contribute to myeloid-specific MRE activation and IFNB1 de-repression.
MRE-centered 3D reorganization of a dedicated IFNB1 regulatory domain upon sumoylation inhibition
The MRE exerts its enhancer activity on IFNB1 from ∼100-kb distance upon SUMOi, suggesting the involvement of DNA looping. Additionally, the H3K9me3 mark has previously been associated with distal-acting silencer elements. To determine if MRE function involves 3D genome organization, both when repressed and active, we reanalyzed available Hi-C data and generated 4C-seq (Circular Chromosome Conformation Capture) data using IFNB1, the MRE, and SIRE-1 as viewpoints in BlaER1 monocytes (Fig. 6A and B and Supplementary Fig. S5). Genomic profiling for CTCF binding and both repressive H3K27me3 and active H3K4me3 marks were performed in parallel. The Hi-C contact map in a 2.5-Mb interval around the IFNB1 gene revealed a number of CTCF-occupied boundaries, indicating a division into topologically associating domains (Fig. 6A and C). Incorporation of the 4C-seq data from control BlaER1 monocytes revealed a further level of organization that is partially uncoupled from CTCF binding. Elevated 4C-seq interactions were observed within a domain of ∼650 kb around IFNB1 (Fig. 6A and B and Supplementary Fig. S5: region in-between the 5′- and 3′- boundaries). The 5′-boundary of the domain overlaps with a CTCF-marked Hi-C boundary that is close to the MLLT3 promoter. The 3′- boundary, visible both in Hi-C and 4C-seq, localizes downstream from IFNA14, yet without noticeable CTCF binding or overlap with a promoter. Within the domain, interactions between the MRE, the repressed IFNB1 gene and the SIRE-1 regulatory element are visible, with a sharp drop in contacts that coincides with a CTCF-independent Hi-C boundary directly downstream from the SIRE-1 element (Fig. 6A–C, dashed line and red arrow, and Supplementary Fig. S5). When repressed, the MRE thus participates in a pre-formed chromatin architecture within a dedicated IFNB1 regulatory domain.
Treatment of BlaER1 monocytes with TAK-981 causes various changes within the IFNB1 domain. Our 4C-seq analysis revealed that the domain remains nonetheless demarcated by the 5′- and 3′- boundaries. Intriguingly though, de novo peaks of MORC3 appear at both boundaries (Fig. 6C, purple arrows). Moreover, all 4C-seq viewpoints display a highly significant increase in contacts towards the downstream side of the IFNB1 domain and beyond, with the MRE acting as boundary for this reorientation (Fig. 6B and D and Supplementary Fig. S5). This SUMOi-induced boundary function coincides with the loss of H3.3 and H3K9me3, the increase in H3K27ac and repression-incompetent MORC3, but not de novo binding of CTCF (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S6A). The domain-wide chromatin reorganization caused increased contacts between the MRE, the now active IFNB1 gene and the SIRE-1 element, albeit relatively minor. In summary, the transition from inactive to active at the MRE does not involve the formation or reinforcement of specific chromatin loops, but rather functions by increasing insulation between the up- and downstream sides of the IFNB1 domain (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S5).
Considering these domain-wide changes, we wondered if gene activation by the MRE could extend beyond its IFNB1 target and MLLT3. RNA-seq experiments in TAK-981-treated BlaER1 cells revealed increased transcription of several genes downstream of the MRE, including HACD4, IFNW1, IFNA4, and IFNA8, together with a long non-coding RNA encompassing the IFNB1 gene in reverse orientation (Fig. 6E). RT-qPCR confirmed that the MRE was required for SUMOi-induced expression of these transcripts (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Fig. S6B). MRE-dependent transcriptional activation is thus limited to genes within the 650-kb regulatory IFNB1 domain and the IFNA8 gene that is localized in the domain directly downstream (Fig. 6F). To confirm the direct involvement of the MRE in chromatin reorganization, we performed 4C-seq in other contexts, which were all in a STAT1-/-STAT2-/- background. Inhibiting sumoylation revealed a comparable MRE boundary effect as in WT cells, confirming that the STAT double-deletion in BlaER1 monocytes does not noticeably influence reorganization dynamics (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). Conversely, upon LPS stimulation, the reorientation of contacts upon IFNB1 activation is not observed, indicating that the MRE boundary function is specific for SUMOi-triggered IFNB1 de-repression (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). Similarly, in the absence of the MRE, the reorientation of contacts upon SUMOi is drastically reduced (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). Combined, we identify the MRE as an atypical regulatory element that, in addition to enhancer activation, gains insulator functions upon sumoylation inhibition to restructure the 3D organization of the extended IFNB1 regulatory domain.

INTRODUCTION:
Introduction
Post-translational modification by the small SUMO protein is an essential regulatory pathway which plays an important role in many normal and pathological biological processes such as cancer, neurological disorders, and infections by pathogens. Three well-characterized functional SUMO paralogs exist in mammals, SUMO1, SUMO2, and SUMO3, of which the latter two (commonly termed SUMO2/3) are highly similar. In mammals, SUMO2/3 is prone to form poly-SUMO chains, while SUMO1 rather functions as a monomer or a chain terminator. Along with this, paralogue-specific functions have been described for SUMO1 and SUMO2/3. In many cases, SUMO, through interactions with SUMO interaction motifs (SIMs)-containing proteins, acts as a glue to physically stabilize multiprotein complexes. Modification by SUMO, which predominantly targets transcription factors and chromatin-associated complexes, is a key regulator of chromatin structure and function, which, in most cases, exerts an inhibitory effect on gene expression [, ].
The SUMO pathway has recently emerged as an important regulator of immunity and host defense against infection by bacterial and viral pathogens. Remarkably, despite the existence of hundreds of SUMO-modified proteins and the pleiotropic role of sumoylation, repression of innate immunity represents the main function of this pathway in myeloid cells. Indeed, genetic inactivation of this pathway results in a strikingly monomorphic transcriptional outcome, which is characterized by a potent activation of basal type I interferon (IFN1) signaling, primarily derived from monocytes. In addition, pathogenic stimulation both exacerbates the IFN1 response and triggers inflammation. The clinical relevance of these findings was underscored with the recent finding that the selective chemical sumoylation inhibitor (SUMOi) TAK-981, which is currently studied in clinical trials in patients with solid tumors, reactivates IFN1 signaling in myeloid cells, promoting antitumor immunity through stimulation of both innate and adaptive immunity in pre-clinical mouse models. Remarkably, blocking IFN1 signaling entirely abrogates SUMOi antitumor activity, highlighting the central role played by the IFN1 pathway in SUMOi efficacy.
Repression of the IFN1 response is achieved by both SUMO2 and SUMO3 paralogs, but not by SUMO1, and, most remarkably, is independent of all known canonical IFN-stimulating pathways. Mechanistically, SUMOi-mediated de-repression of IFN1 signaling entirely relies on induction of IFNB1 transcription. Indeed, whereas the potent basal IFNB1 activation incurred by loss of sumoylation is preserved in IFNAR1-/-IFNAR2-/- cells, expression of interferon stimulated genes is almost completely abrogated. A SUMOi-sensitive genomic element (SIRE-1, also called ICE/L2), located 15–20 kb upstream of the IFNB1 promoter was recently found to regulate IFNB1 expression upon innate immune stimuli in both murine and human cells. Yet, the precise molecular mechanisms that explain the spontaneous IFNB1 response induced by loss of sumoylation remain poorly understood. Notably, both the SUMO-repressed regulatory element(s) and sumoylated protein(s) involved in basal IFNB1 activity are unknown.
To gain insight into the role of sumoylation in innate immunity, we analyzed the endogenous SUMO2/3 proteomes in murine myeloid cells activated by innate immune stimuli or not. While the sumoylome appears remarkably stable following activation, the H2A.Z-loading Tip60 complex emerged as one of the main chromatin complexes showing dynamic sumoylation upon stimulation. In unstimulated cells, we identified the mouse ATPase protein MORC3 as the most heavily sumoylated substrate. We show that sumoylation operates from a unique MORC3-regulated element (MRE) that controls myeloid-specific IFN1 response. The MRE, owing to its repeated nature, accumulates multiple binding sites for the myeloid-enriched PU.1 pioneer factor that is both able to activate the MRE and to confer SUMOi-responsiveness to nonimmune cells. Inhibiting sumoylation leads to both genomic architecture remodeling and MRE epigenetic reprogramming. The MRE thus acquires insulator functions within a large IFNB1 regulatory domain together with enhancer activity through removal of an atypical H3.3/H3K9me3 heterochromatin state combined with recruitment of PU.1, leading to IFNB1 activation. We suggest a mechanism whereby myeloid-specific PU.1 hyperactivation of a unique tandem repeat coupled with loss of repression drives SUMOi-mediated IFNB1 activation at a distance.
