ABSTRACT:
Lysosomes comprise the main degradative compartment of almost all mammalian cells and are involved in various cellular functions, most of which are catalyzed by the lysosomal proteome. Lysosomal proteins are low abundant, complicating their analysis by mass spectrometry-based proteomics. To increase analytical performance, and to enable profiling of lysosomal content, lysosomes are frequently enriched, for which currently two approaches are most commonly used: superparamagnetic nanoparticles (SPIONs) and immunoprecipitation from cells overexpressing a 3xHA-tagged version of TMEM192 (TMEM-IP). We investigated the effects of both approaches on the lysosomal proteome through enrichment of lysosomes with the respective other approach proteomic analyses. For SPIONs treatment, we identified mainly an effect on cellular iron homeostasis, while changes of the lysosomal proteome were moderate. For overexpression of TMEM192, we observed more pronounced effects in lysosomal protein expression, especially related to alterations of membrane proteins and such involved in protein trafficking. Furthermore, we established a combined strategy consisting of sequential lysosome enrichment with SPIONs and TMEM-IPs. This approach enabled increased purity of lysosome enriched fractions and, through TMEM-IP-based lysosome enrichment from SPIONs flow through and eluate fractions, further characterization of the individual approaches’ properties.

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
For each sample, 10 µg of protein were digested using single-pot, solid-phase-enhanced sample preparation (SP3) as described previously29 with some modifications. Briefly, samples were reduced with 22 mM DTT (30 min at 56 °C) and alkylated with 42 mM acrylamide (30 min at RT), followed by quenching of the reaction with 22 mM DTT (10 min at RT). Subsequently, samples were incubated with a 1:1 mixture of beads (#45152105050250 and #65152105050250, GE Healthcare, Illinois, USA) in a protein to bead ratio of 1:10 (w/w), followed by addition of 96% ethanol and incubation in a thermomixer for 5 min at 1,000 rpm and 24 °C. Sample tubes were placed into a magnetic rack, supernatants removed, beads washed three times with 80% ethanol, and digested with r-LysC (Promega, Mannheim, Germany) at an enzyme to protein ratio of 1:100 (w/w) in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) for 2 hours at 1,000 rpm, 37 °C in a thermomixer. Subsequently, sequencing grade trypsin (Promega) was added at an enzyme to protein ratio of 1:25 (w/w) and samples were digested overnight in a thermomixer at 1,000 rpm, 37 °C. The next day, beads were pelleted using a magnetic stand and the supernatant containing the peptides were transferred into a fresh tube, desalted using stop and go extraction (STAGE) tips,30 and dried using a vacuum centrifuge.  Peptides were resuspended in 5% acetonitrile (ACN)/5% formic acid (FA) and analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 nano-UHPLC coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (both Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Chromatographic separation was performed using in-house-produced columns, prepared by packing 45 cm spray tips generated from 360 µm outer diameter/100µM inner diameter fused silica capillaries with a P-2000 laser puller (Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA) with 3 µm ReprosilPur AQ C18 particles (Dr. Maisch, Ammerbuch-Entringen, Germany). Samples were loaded directly to the analytical column at a flow rate of 600 nl/min in 100% solvent A (0.1% FA), followed by separation with a linear gradient from 5-35% solvent B (95%ACN, 0.1% FA) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. For library generation runs by data dependent acquisition (DDA), individual replicates were combined in equal ratios and analyzed with a 240 min linear gradient. MS1 scans were acquired from m/z 375-1500 in the Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 60,000 followed by fragmentation of the most abundant 2+ to 4+ precursor ions in the top speed mode (cycle time: 5 sec). Precursor isolation was performed in the quadrupole with an isolation width of m/z 1.6, ions were fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a collision energy of 30%, and MS2 spectra acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer with a resolution of 30,000 and a dynamic exclusion window of 120 sec. The automatic gain control (AGC) and maximum injection time were set to 4 x 105/5 x 105 and 50 ms/54 ms for MS1/MS2 scans, respectively. Data independent acquisition (DIA) runs were performed with linear gradients of 120 min. Following one MS1 scan from m/z 350-1200, 36 consecutive HCD MS2 scans at 30 % collision energy were performed with an isolation window of m/z 24.1 (overlap of m/z 0.5). Both MS1 and MS2 scans were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer with a resolution of 120,000 and 30,000, respectively. For MS1/MS2 scans, the AGC and maximum injection time were set to 5 x 105/1 x 106 and 20 ms/60 ms, respectively, resulting in a total cycle time of 3.44 sec.

DATA PROCESSING:
Thermo *.raw files were analyzed with Spectronaut 15.6.20. Hybrid spectral libraries were generated from both DDA and DIA files utilizing 3 to 6 fragment ions for each peptide with the Pulsar search engine incorporated in Spectronaut and UniProt Homo sapiens (released 06/2019 with 21,155 entries). The deep learning-assisted indexed retention time (iRT) regression was used as a default setting for retention time alignment.31 For database searching, the following parameters were defined: enzyme: trypsin/P; number of allowed miss cleavage sites: 2; fixed modification: propionamide at cysteine; variable modifications: acetylation at protein N-termini and oxidation of methionine. Peptide and protein identification were performed at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%. For peptide/protein quantification, a q-value cutoff of 0.01 and 0.05, respectively, was applied and missing value imputation was disabled.  Further analysis and data visualization were performed using R (v. 4.3.1),32 MS Excel 2016, GraphPad Prism v9. (GraphPad Software) and g:GOSt.33

ABSTRACT (PubMed):

Most of them are catalyzed by lysosomal proteins, which typically are low abundant, complicating their analysis by mass spectrometry-based proteomics. To increase analytical performance and to enable profiling of lysosomal content, lysosomes are often enriched. Two approaches have gained popularity in recent years, namely, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) and immunoprecipitation from cells overexpressing a 3xHA-tagged version of TMEM192 (TMEM-IP). The effect of these approaches on the lysosomal proteome has not been investigated to date. We addressed this topic through a combination of both techniques and proteomic analysis of lysosome-enriched fractions. For SPIONs treatment, we identified altered cellular iron homeostasis and moderate changes of the lysosomal proteome. For overexpression of TMEM192, we observed more pronounced effects in lysosomal protein expression, especially for lysosomal membrane proteins and those involved in protein trafficking. This enabled increased purity of lysosome-enriched fractions and, through TMEM-IP-based lysosome enrichment from SPIONs flow-through and eluate fractions, additional insights into the properties of individual approaches.
