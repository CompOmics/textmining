ABSTRACT:
Specific protein binders with high affinity, such as antibodies or nanobodies, are scarce for most solute carrier (SLCs) transporters. Moreover, general approaches that can systematically generate binders for multi-transmembrane proteins, such as SLCs, channels or GPCRs, are still missing. Purifying and reconstituting transmembrane proteins in their lipidic environments remain challenging, hence membrane proteins normally act as poor input material for binder generation. To overcome this challenge, we first identified state-of-the-art methods to generate protein binders targeting membrane transporters. Next, we produced full length protein or cell lines as input material for 27 SLCs, which were then processed in the selected binder generation platforms. As a result, we obtained >300 binders for 23 SLCs, 1 SLC failed and 3 SLCs are still in process. Then we established a cell-based validation workflow using immunofluorescent and immunoprecipitation methods to process all obtained binders. To further highlight the suitability of high-performance binders as an important tool for the biomolecular characterization of SLCs, we tested whether three SLC12A6 binders could be used in a quantitative co-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS) approach. This initial analysis of the IP-MS dataset showed that SLC12A6 specific binders are capable of strongly enriching SLC12A6. We also showed that despite high sequence similarity, the different binders showed different binder to target ratios, indicating different affinities to SLC12A6 in cell lysates. With this work, we were able to generate easily renewable and highly specific binders against SLCs, which will greatly facilitate the study of this neglected protein family.

PROJECT DESCRIPTION:
Protein binder based IP-MS of SLC12A6 (KCC3) in Hek293WT OE cell lines

SAMPLE PROCESSING:
For the Immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-MS) experiment,  HEK293 JI SLC12A6 WTOE cells were split in 15 cm plate and fusion protein expression was induced by addition of 1 µg/ml doxycycline for 24 h. Cell pellets were d lysed in 1 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, 250 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, pH 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail 30 min on ice. Lysates were clarified at 13,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C and protein amount was normalized by Bradford protein assay. To a total of 5 mg protein extract 2 µg of SLC12A6-binder was added and lysates were incubated at 4°C overnight. Next, 10 µl of anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel (Sigma, #A2220) were added to each sample for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were transferred to filter columns (Bio-Rad) and were washed twice with 500 µl lysis buffer with detergent followed by washing them twice with 500 µL of lysis buffer without detergent. Enriched proteins were eluted by 50 µl of freshly prepared 8 M urea (Sigma-Aldrich) in 50 mM ammonium-bi-carbonate (Ambic, Sigma-Aldrich) pH 8.8 and samples were incubated for 30 min at 600 rpm at RT. Next, IP-samples were diluted to 0.8 M urea by addition of 50 mM Ambic. Reduction was performed by addition of DTT to a final concentration of 5 mM and incubation at 37 °C for 30 min at 600 rpm. Alkylation of Cysteine residues was achieved by addition of IAA to a final concentration of 10 nM followed by incubation ofsamples in the dark for 30 min at RT at 600 rpm. Proteins were digested by of 0.625 µg of Trypsin/Lys-C mix (Promega) and incubation at 37°C at 600 rpm overnight. Samples were acidified to pH 2-3 and peptides were cleaned up by reversed phase C18 clean-up procedure. In short, UltraMicroSpin (Silica C18, 3 – 30 µg capacity, #SUM SS18V, The Nerst Group) were activated by 2x 200 µl MeOH and 2 x 200 µl ACN (Sigma). Columns were equilibrated by the addition of 3x 200 µl 0.1% TFA in H2O (VWR Chemicals). Peptides were loaded and washed with 5x 200 µL of 0.1% TFA in H2O. Peptides were eluted by a total of 200 µl 50 µl 60% ACN with 0.4% Formic acid (FA, Sigma), dried in the speed-vac and stored at -80 °C till data acquisition. For the For AP-Ms the same SL12A6 cell line and a cell line expressing GFP-SH as negative control were used. The TC procedure was the same, except that for each sample 2x 15 cm dishes were used. Cells were lysed in lysis under native conditions. After clarification, 150 µl of 50 % slurry of StrepTactin Sepharose beads (IBA) were added to the sample (2h at 4°C). Beads were washed with 2x 1 ml of lysis buffer, resuspended in detergent free lysis buffer and transferred to filter columns. Bound protein complexes were eluted by addition 2% SDS in 50 mM HEPES pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA followed by incubation on shaker for 15 min, 25 °C 1000 rpm. Proteins were reduced by DTT (10 mM) and alkylated by IAA (10 mM). The detergent was removed by SP3 procedure (magnetic carboxylate modified particles, GE Healthcare). Proteins were digested to peptides by the addition of 1 µg of trypsin (Promega)/ Lys-C-mix diluted in 100 µl 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer on a shaker overnight at 37 °C at 600 rpm. Protocol is available under https://zenodo.org/records/7457416.  All MS data were acquired on a QE (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For IP-MS samples, peptides were resuspended in 18 µL MS-buffer (0.1% TFA in H2O, spiked with 16.66 fmol/µl SH-quant isotopic labelled peptides ([NH2]AADITS(L)Y(K)[COOH]: Heavy Leu: 13C(6)15N(1)[+7] and heavy C-term Lys: 13C(6)15N(2)[+8] or only heavy Leu). AP-MS samples were reconstituted in 30 µL MS-buffer. MS-data were acquired a QE interfaced with a nano-flow HPLC (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in DDA mode. Peptides were loaded onto a trap column (Pepmap 100 5 µm, 5 × 0.3 mm, ThermoFisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 10 µL/min using 0.1% TFA as loading buffer. Peptides were separated over a 120 min analytical gradient (4 to 24% of 90 % ACN with 0.4 % FA within 24 min, 24 to 36% of 90 % ACN with 0.4 % FA within 12 min and, 36 to 100% of 90 % ACN with 0.4 % FA within 1 min, 100% of 90 % ACN with 0.4 % FA for 6 min before equilibrating at 4% of 90 % ACN with 0.4 % FA for 18 min) operated at a constant flow rate of 230 nL/min. During data acquisition the in-house packed analytical column (50 cm, 75 µm inner diameter analytical column packed in house with ReproSil-Pur 120 C18-AQ, 3 µm) kept at a constant temperature of 40 °C (Sonation column oven PRSO-V1). Peptides were ionized at 1.8 Kv (Nanospray Flex™). MS1 scans: 375 - 1650 m/z in the OT, at a resolution of 70,000, AGC target of 1 × 106 and 55 ms max. injection time. TOP15 most intense peptides were selected for fragmentation. Fragmentation was achieved by HCD at a norm. collision energy of 28%. MS2 scans: AGC target of 1 × 105  and 110 ms max. injection time, a resolution of 17,50. The lock mass was at m/z 445.120024 and dynamic exclusion was set to 40 s. MS acquisition protocol: https://zenodo.org/records/7462253.

DATA PROCESSING:
Peptide identification and quantification was performed employing Philosopher (v3.4.13) (da Veiga Leprevost F, Nat Method 2020). Raw files were converted to vendor independent mzML format (MSconvert (3.0.21128-7376ae988)) and files were searched by MSFragger (version 3.3.). As protein database the consensus sequences from UniProtKB (20,401 protein sequences, downloaded on the 12/09/2022) supplemented with the three binder sequences was used. Decoys were by reverting the sequences. Search parameters in short: fully tryptic peptides, with up to 2 missed cleavages, static modification carbamidomethylation on cysteine residues, variable modifications oxidation on methionine and N-terminal acetylation, max. 5 modifications per peptide. MS1 mass tolerance of 50 ppm and a tolerance of 20 ppm for fragment ions. Peptide length from 5 to 63 amino acids, and a MS1 m/z range of 375 to 5000 m/z with a precursor charge state of+1 to +4. LFQ was performed with freequant using default parameters. Peptide and protein FDR was controlled at 1% using PeptideProphet (v5.2.1) and ProteinProphet. The IP-MS and AP-MS data of SLC12A6 and the GFP negative controls were further analyzed to derive the protein interactome for each bait protein respectively for each binder. For this additional 29 negative controls form CRAPome and 6 controls of a high pH fractionated sample were supplemented to the dataset. Subsequently we used the probabilistic scoring tool SAINT with default settings (primary score (FC-A) with average and 10 virtual controls, and SAINT with average and 10 virtual controls, a n-burn of 2000 with 4000 iterations and a minimal fold change of 1 and normalization with 1). Lastly, we filtered the interactome to differentiate background from true protein interactions and annotated most probable contaminants using the CRAPome frequency table. Only interactions with an average probability of larger or equal to 0.95, a FC of larger or equal to 2, a BFDR of smaller or equal to 0.01 were considered as interactors. Within the IP-MS dataset thresholds were slightly relaxed due to the overall lower abundance of proteins. The obtained data was used for GO enrichment analysis and visualization in Cytoscape. IP-MS was further compared to AP-MS obtained protein interactome. Quantitative data extraction of target and binder sequences  was performed by Skyline (v.23.1.0.268). In addition the Step-HA (SH-tag) peptide and heavy SH-quant* peptide (+15) were quantified within Skyline. Unique sequences were extracted after performing an in-silico digestion of binders and target protein by iPEP (settings for LTQ Orbitrap). Overlapping peptide sequences of binder proteins were excluded. Overlapping peptide sequences of SLC12A6 target with close homologs such as SLC12A4 and SLC12A7 were excluded. In total up to 6 peptides were extracted per protein identifier. Peptide signals were normalized by heavy spike-in SH-quant peptide to account for differences in MS-performance. Protein abundance was derived by TOP3 highest intensity peptides across the experiment.
